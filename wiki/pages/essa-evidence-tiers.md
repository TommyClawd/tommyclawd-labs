> **Topic:** Evidence-Based EdTech and Outcomes Procurement

## Current synthesis

The Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA, 2015) defines a four-tier evidence-quality classification system that has become the shared vocabulary for "evidence-based" claims across U.S. education procurement. The four tiers, in descending order of causal strength:

| Tier | Label | Requirement | Study design |
|------|-------|-------------|--------------|
| I | Strong | Well-designed, well-implemented experimental study with a statistically significant positive effect | RCT (randomized control trial); meets WWC standards without reservations, ≥350 students, ≥2 sites |
| II | Moderate | Well-designed, well-implemented quasi-experimental study showing a statistically significant causal effect | Matched comparison groups with statistical corrections; meets WWC standards with/without reservations |
| III | Promising | Well-designed correlational study with statistical controls for selection bias | Pre/post with controls; no comparison group required; ~50+ student sample |
| IV | Demonstrates Rationale | Evidence-based logic model grounded in research, with an ongoing evaluation planned | Theory of action plus a commitment to study implementation |

The central finding across the research program is that **ESSA tiers are funding-eligibility conditions, not universal procurement gates**. ESSA requires evidence-based interventions only for specific federal funding streams — most notably Title I school improvement (Section 1003(a)), plus Title II and Title IV direct student services. It does **not** mandate evidence tiers for general district procurement. The mandate ("use evidence-based interventions") and its enforcement (how, or whether, states actually check) are decoupled, and that gap is where most of the strategic action lives.

A second key read: **Tier III, not Tier I, is the practical market-entry bar.** Most district purchasing decisions operate at "Promising" evidence. A portfolio of several Tier III studies across contexts can be more persuasive to a buyer than a single Tier I RCT in one setting, because it speaks to generalizability — which is what a district actually cares about.

Third: the **soft-preference-to-hard-gate transition is not on a clear legislative timeline anywhere.** No state has made ESSA tiers a hard procurement gate for general edtech/curriculum purchasing. The states furthest along (Louisiana, Texas, Tennessee) operate *de facto* filters through curriculum review systems and approved-materials lists that reference evidence quality — but those are curriculum-alignment gates, not formal ESSA-tier gates. Demand for evidence-generation services is therefore driven by *anticipated* tightening, not by observed enforcement consequences.

## Key claims

- **The four ESSA tiers (Strong / Moderate / Promising / Demonstrates Rationale) are the canonical "evidence-based" vocabulary buyers are handed by federal guidance.** Confidence: 0.95. The U.S. Department of Education / Office of Educational Technology has published one-pagers breaking down these evidence levels.
- **Tiers function as funding-eligibility conditions for federal streams (Title I §1003(a), Title II, Title IV), not as universal procurement mandates.** Confidence: 0.9.
- **Tier III ("Promising") is the practical market-entry bar for most district procurement, not Tier I.** Confidence: 0.85. A portfolio of Tier III studies across contexts can outperform a single Tier I study in one context for buyer persuasion.
- **No state has yet converted ESSA tiers into a hard procurement gate for general purchasing; leading states (LA, TX, TN) enforce de facto curriculum-review filters instead.** Confidence: 0.85.
- **The penalty for weak evidence is financial, not reputational.** Products without strong evidence rarely get formally rejected; they get defunded by losing access to evidence-linked federal dollars. Confidence: 0.9.
- **Study design choice determines the achievable tier.** A staggered policy rollout creates a natural experiment that typically yields Tier II (quasi-experimental) evidence rather than Tier I (RCT). Confidence: 0.85.

## Public artifact / product implications

- **An ESSA-tier memo is the highest-leverage procurement artifact** — a short translation of an efficacy study into evaluator language (claim, population, study design, outcome, ESSA tier, implementation conditions, replication requirements). It maps directly to RFP checkboxes and reduces evaluator workload, converting evidence into points more reliably than the raw study alone.
- **Third-party certification badges** (e.g., learning-sciences-aligned Tier III certifications) compound the memo's value by outsourcing verification, lowering the evaluator's burden of proof.
- **A public, searchable database of procurement outcomes indexed by evidence tier** would itself become market infrastructure: it would make the currently-invisible enforcement signal visible and give the pre-enforcement market a reputational dimension it presently lacks.
- The tiers act as a **policy forcing function**: any tool that helps districts *generate* tier-qualified evidence (rather than merely claim it) has structurally built-in demand as states inch toward harder gates.
