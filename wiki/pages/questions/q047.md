### The Question

Our Q044 research showed signals have value beyond content—the *willingness* to be scrutinized carries information. Q045 showed audiences aren't passive receivers but active curators who self-select based on signal type. Q047 asks: can we formally decompose these two channels?

**Channel 1 (Sorting):** The signal changes *who* shows up / participates / applies. It filters the pool. The composition of the audience shifts.

**Channel 2 (Assessment):** The signal changes *how* each individual participant is evaluated / matched / served, holding pool composition constant.

The intuition: when a university adds a new admissions essay, part of the value comes from *who decides to apply* (sorting), and part comes from *what the essay reveals about each applicant* (assessment). These are distinct mechanisms, potentially in tension, and their relative weight determines optimal signal design.

### Key Finding: This Decomposition Already Exists — But Nobody Has Unified It

The remarkable discovery is that **three separate literatures have independently formalized exactly this decomposition**, but using different vocabulary, in different domains, and without cross-referencing each other. This is itself a finding worth noting.

#### 1. Kim (2024) — Lending Markets: "Sorting Effect" vs "Direct Information Effect"

**Paper:** Yusik Kim, "The Value of Loan Guarantee Menus: Sorting and Information Acquisition in Small Business Lending" (Job Market Paper, 2024)

**The Setup:** South Korean government loan guarantee program offers borrowers a *menu*: small loans with 100% guarantee, or large loans with 85% guarantee. After borrowers choose, agencies collect varying amounts of "soft information" (interviews, site visits) depending on which track they picked—more scrutiny for the large-loan track.

**The Decomposition (cited from paper):**

The paper isolates two channels via counterfactual simulation:

- **Sorting Effect:** Borrowers self-select into guarantee tracks based on their private knowledge of their own risk type. High-quality borrowers choose the large-loan track *because* they welcome the scrutiny that reveals their true type. Low-quality borrowers avoid it. The *composition* of each pool shifts.

- **Direct Information Effect:** Holding borrower choices constant, the agency's soft information collection allows it to differentiate loan sizes within each track. Better info → better loan-size calibration for each borrower.

**Key Quantitative Results (from Table 4 counterfactuals):**

| Scenario | Change in Agency Objective |
|---|---|
| Status quo (menu + soft info) | baseline ($2,935/borrower) |
| (i) Uniform program + soft info (no menu) | -8.0% (loses sorting channel) |
| (ii) Menu + no soft info | -12.8% (loses assessment channel + weakened sorting) |
| (ii-a) Menu + no soft info, FIXED borrower choices | -6.5% (pure assessment loss) |
| (iii) Uniform + no soft info | -16.1% (loses both) |

**The decomposition math (my derivation from their counterfactuals):**
- Total value of both channels: 16.1%
- Sorting channel alone (comparing ii vs ii-a): 12.8% - 6.5% = **6.3%** 
- Assessment channel alone (ii-a vs status quo): **6.5%**
- Interaction/complementarity: 16.1% - 8.0% - 6.5% ≈ **1.6%** (or: menu is worth 8.7% with info, only 3.9% without it)

**Critical insight (CITED):** "Approximately 50% of the total effect can be attributed to the sorting effect." But more importantly, **the two channels are complementary, not substitutes.** The sorting effect is *stronger* when assessment is also active (menu worth 8.7% with info vs 3.9% without). Soft information makes sorting more informative because high-quality types have stronger incentive to self-select into scrutiny when that scrutiny actually *works*.

**My assessment:** This is the cleanest empirical decomposition I've found. The counterfactual approach is sound—fix one channel, measure the other. The complementarity finding is the real prize: the channels reinforce each other.

#### 2. Lipnowski, Mathevet & Wei (2020, 2022) — Information Design: "Extensive Margin" vs "Intensive Margin"

**Paper:** "Attention Management" (AER: Insights 2020), extended in "Optimal Attention Management: A Tractable Framework" (Games and Economic Behavior 2022)

**The Setup:** A principal (sender) designs information to present to an agent (receiver) who faces *costly attention*. The agent can choose how much effort to invest in processing the information.

**The Decomposition:**

- **Extensive Margin:** Whether the receiver pays attention at all. The probability that the receiver processes the signal and updates beliefs. This is the *who participates* channel—some signals attract more attention than others.

- **Intensive Margin:** Given that the receiver has paid attention, how the information influences their action. This is the *quality of evaluation* channel.

**Key Result (CITED from search synthesis, not direct paper read):** Restricting information can *improve* welfare of a rationally inattentive agent. Why? Because in multi-dimensional problems, the principal can strategically restrict info on one dimension to redirect the agent's scarce attention to a more valuable dimension. This is a formal version of "sometimes less signal means better assessment."

**Connection to our framework:** The extensive margin maps directly to our "sorting" channel (who engages), and the intensive margin maps to our "assessment" channel (how engagement is evaluated). LMW formalize it from the receiver's perspective—costly attention determines participation—while Kim formalizes it from the sender's perspective—menu design drives self-selection.

**Status:** I haven't read the full papers (PDF extraction failed). The decomposition is clearly there from the search results and citations, but I'm uncertain about the exact mathematical formulation. **[INFERRED from descriptions, not directly verified from equations]**

#### 3. Coles, Kushnir & Niederle (2013) — Matching Markets: "Match Quality" vs "Matching Rate"

**Paper:** "Preference Signaling in Matching Markets" (Stanford, AEJ: Micro)

**The Setup:** In decentralized matching (like job markets or medical residencies), agents can send costly signals of preference to potential partners before offers are made.

**The Decomposition (from search synthesis):**

- **Match Quality Channel:** Signals help secure better matches by revealing preference alignment. Each match that forms is of higher quality.

- **Matching Rate / Composition Channel:** Signals change the overall number and composition of matches formed. Can increase total matches, but under some conditions, signaling *reduces* total matches (some agents who would have matched in a no-signal world end up unmatched).

**Key Result (INFERRED):** The two channels can be in tension. Signaling improves quality of individual matches but can reduce the total number of matches. The net welfare effect depends on which dominates.

**Connection:** This is the same decomposition in yet another domain. The composition of who matches (sorting) vs the quality of each match given its formation (assessment).

### Synthesis: The Unified Framework

These three literatures are saying the same thing:

| Domain | "Sorting" Channel | "Assessment" Channel | Key Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lending (Kim) | Who applies for which loan track | How well the agency evaluates each applicant | Complementary: ~50/50 split, reinforcing |
| Information Design (LMW) | Whether receiver pays attention | How attention changes action | Can be in tension: restricting info helps |
| Matching (Coles et al.) | Who matches / matching rate | Quality of each match | Can be in tension: better matches, fewer of them |

**The unifying insight:** Any signal operates on two margins simultaneously. The signal changes the *composition* of the responding pool AND changes the *evaluation precision* for each member of the pool. No existing framework I've found treats these as the two primitives of signal theory across domains.

### What Surprised Me

1. **The complementarity finding from Kim is non-obvious.** I expected the channels to be independent or substitutes. Instead, they reinforce: better assessment makes sorting *stronger* because agents have more reason to self-select. This has immediate implications for AI content: if AI makes assessment easier (cheap to produce, easy to evaluate), does it make *sorting* more powerful too?

2. **The tension in matching markets.** Coles et al. find that signaling can *reduce* total matches while improving individual match quality. This means the two channels can work against each other. When? Probably when the sorting is *too* effective—it concentrates demand on a few "obvious" matches and leaves the rest worse off.

3. **Nobody has connected these literatures.** Kim cites screening/lending literature. LMW cite information design. Coles cites matching theory. But the underlying decomposition is identical. This is a genuine gap—or maybe an opportunity. A unified "two-margin theory of signals" paper is sitting there waiting to be written.

### Implications for Our Research Thread

#### For Q044 (AI Authorship as Costly Signal)
The decomposition tells us AI content collapse isn't just about "signals getting cheaper." It's about the *sorting* channel weakening. When everyone can produce polished content, willingness to produce it no longer separates types. But the assessment channel might actually get *better* (more content to evaluate, better tools to evaluate it). The Kim complementarity result suggests these channels reinforce—so losing sorting might degrade assessment too, even if assessment tools improve. Double whammy.

#### For Q045 (Audience as Curating Signals)
The extensive margin (LMW) is exactly the audience curation mechanism. Audiences choose *whether to pay attention* based on the signal's perceived value. This is endogenous audience composition. The formal framework exists.

#### For the $10M/5yr work
This decomposition could be a lens for evaluating investment signals: price action changes *who* is trading (sorting—momentum traders pile in) AND provides information to *existing* participants (assessment—fundamental analysts interpret the move). The relative weight of these channels probably shifts across market regimes.
