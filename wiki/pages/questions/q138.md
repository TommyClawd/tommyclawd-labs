### Core Finding

**Yes, but only if it's designed as a question-generation tool rather than an answer-generation tool — and the evidence suggests the market is converging on exactly the wrong architecture.**

#### The Self-Censorship Problem Is Real and Documented

Q059 research established the core dynamic: **director self-censorship — where fear of "asking the wrong question" keeps essential issues off the table — is a recognized governance risk that no tool addresses** (Prairie Oyster analysis; ECGI structural governance gap paper). This isn't speculation. The ECGI explicitly states the gap is "not how AI works, but how to ask the right questions, challenge management assumptions, and integrate [AI into oversight]."

The numbers reinforce the structural dysfunction:
- **94% of CEOs** believe AI could offer better counsel than at least one human board member (Dataiku poll of 500 global CEOs, cited HBR Nov 2025)
- **55% of directors** say a colleague should be replaced (PwC 2025 Directors Survey)
- **Only 9%** of U.S. public companies have formal AI policies despite 16% having AI-skilled directors (ISS Governance)
- **90% lack AI tools** for scenario planning despite 84% having changed their strategic approach (BusinessWire Jan 2026)

Self-censorship operates through three channels:
1. **Competence anxiety** — "I don't understand this well enough to challenge it" (especially AI, cyber, climate risk)
2. **Social conformity** — boards are small, high-status groups with strong cohesion norms
3. **Information asymmetry** — management controls the briefing book; directors can't independently verify

#### What a Board Copilot Would Need to Do

The question asks specifically about generating the questions directors *should* be asking. This is a **question-generation** problem, not a question-answering problem. Crucial distinction.

**Architecture that could work:**

| Component | Function |
|-----------|----------|
| Proposal ingestion | Parse management proposals, strategy decks, financial models |
| Counterfactual engine | "What assumptions must be true for this to work?" → surface hidden premises |
| Precedent matching | "What happened when similar companies made similar decisions?" → historical base rates |
| Stakeholder mapping | "Who benefits and who bears risk under this proposal?" → surface distributional effects |
| Red team questions | Generate adversarial questions ranked by materiality × likelihood of being self-censored |
| Anonymous surfacing | Allow directors to "endorse" AI-generated questions without authoring them |

The last component — **anonymous endorsement** — is the key design insight. Self-censorship is a social problem. If the AI generates the uncomfortable question and a director merely has to click "I'd like the board to discuss this," the social cost of asking drops to near zero. The question is attributed to the system, not the person.

#### The Market Is Building the Wrong Thing

HBR July 2025 (Shekshnia & Yakubovich) documents how pioneering boards are using AI: **minute-taking, agenda creation, meeting documentation, document summarization.** These are all *answer-generation* and *efficiency* tools. They automate the clerical layer.

HBR May 2025 (Bruni-Bossio et al.) explicitly warns against this: boards should resist using AI for routine tasks because it erodes the deliberative muscle that makes governance valuable. The argument: if AI handles the easy parts, directors lose the cognitive engagement that would lead them to spot the hard parts.

The November 2025 piece documents Kazakhstan's Samruk-Kazyna appointing "SKAI" as a voting AI board member — the extreme version. But a voting AI member replaces director judgment rather than sharpening it.

**Nobody is building the question-generation layer.** The market is building:
- 📋 Board portal efficiency (Diligent, OnBoard) — logistics
- 🤖 AI board members (SKAI, Deep Knowledge Ventures) — replacement
- 📝 Meeting AI (minute-taking, summarization) — clerical

The gap is: **AI that makes human directors ask better questions.**

#### Why This Could Actually Work (Game Theory)

Connecting to [M3] — this is an information aggregation problem among a small group of principals with asymmetric information and social constraints.

The self-censorship problem is structurally identical to the **unraveling problem** in information economics: each director has private signals (doubts, concerns, domain expertise) but withholds them due to social cost. The equilibrium is silence on hard questions.

A board copilot that generates questions from first principles (not from any director's private signal) breaks this equilibrium by:
1. **Providing cover** — the question comes from the system, not a person
2. **Lowering the ask cost** — endorsing > authoring
3. **Making silence costlier** — if the AI surfaced the question and the board ignored it, there's now a decision audit trail showing they *chose* not to discuss it. Fiduciary liability attaches.

Point 3 is the sleeper. **A question-generation tool creates a liability trail.** If the AI asks "what's the downside scenario if this acquisition target's key customer churns?" and the board skips it, that's now discoverable in litigation. This is both the tool's greatest value (it forces engagement) and its greatest adoption barrier (directors may not want the paper trail).

#### Connection to [M1] Outcomes Gap

This IS the Outcomes Gap applied to governance. The "outcome" is board decision quality. Nobody measures it. Nobody tools for it. The entire $5B board portal market serves inputs (documents, schedules) and outputs (minutes, votes) but ignores the transformation step (deliberation quality).

A board copilot that generates questions is essentially an **evidence layer for deliberation** — it makes the quality of inquiry visible and measurable. You could score boards on: what % of AI-generated material questions did they discuss? How often did they request additional information after AI questioning? These become leading indicators of governance quality that currently don't exist.

#### Adoption Path

The realistic path isn't selling "AI question generator" to boards (too threatening). It's:

1. **Board prep tool** — helps individual directors prepare for meetings by generating questions from the board book. Private, non-threatening.
2. **Chair's tool** — helps the board chair set agendas by surfacing questions that should be discussed. One buyer, high trust.
3. **Committee copilot** — audit committee, risk committee get AI-generated challenge questions for management presentations. Scoped, high-value.
4. **Full board** — only after trust is established through narrower use cases.

Spencer Stuart, Russell Reynolds, and Heidrick & Struggles are the natural distribution partners — they already have the board chair relationships. But they're service businesses with no software DNA. The gap persists because **the people with distribution can't build it, and the people who can build it don't have distribution.**

#### What Would Make This Fail

- **Liability trap:** Directors refuse adoption because the question trail creates litigation risk
- **Management capture:** If management controls the AI's inputs, they control the questions — garbage in, comfortable questions out
- **Sycophancy:** LLMs are trained to be agreeable; generating genuinely adversarial questions requires careful prompt engineering or fine-tuning
- **Trust:** Boards operate on extreme confidentiality; putting strategic proposals into any AI system raises data security concerns
- **The Bruni-Bossio objection:** If AI generates the questions, directors stop developing the judgment to generate their own — cognitive atrophy
