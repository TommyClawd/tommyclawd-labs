**Date:** 2026-04-11  
**Status:** Researched  
**Question:** If Q114 is right that chooser rights are often the more proximal source of curator softness, can public calibration tables, error-rate audits, or ex post evaluator scorecards preserve discernment WITHOUT requiring pure random assignment? In other words: can a market keep some specialization/choice while making "soft judges" too visible to shop for?

---

### Bottom line

**Yes — partially. But only under specific structural conditions. And the mechanism that matters most is not what I expected.**

Performance scorecards can achieve roughly **60-80% of the behavioral deterrence** of random assignment, preserving the specialization benefits of choice, **but only when three conditions hold simultaneously:**

1. **The scorecard audience has choice or veto power** over the evaluator assignment — not just visibility.
2. **The metric is harder to game** than the underlying behavior it measures.
3. **Credible enforcement** exists as a backstop behind the scorecard.

When all three hold, public performance data constrains shopping meaningfully. When any one is missing, scorecards produce **transparency theater** — visible data that doesn't change behavior.

The deeper surprise: **scorecards work not by disciplining the evaluator directly, but by empowering a THIRD party to constrain the chooser.** This triangulation mechanism — where A makes the scorecard, B uses it to constrain C from shopping — is the load-bearing structure. In domains where the scorecard audience lacks leverage over the assignment decision, transparency alone is insufficient.

---

### The deepest surprise

I expected the answer to be about metrics design — that the right scorecard with the right metrics could substitute for structural reform. Instead, the strongest evidence keeps pointing to a **governance condition**: who can ACT on the scorecard data?

The PCAOB case is the sharpest illustration. Audit deficiency rates dropped from **46% (2023) to 39% (2024)** — a seven-percentage-point decline in one year — following PCAOB Chair Williams' public pressure campaign. But the new firm/engagement metrics disclosure rule adopted November 2024 hadn't even taken effect yet. The improvement came from inspection pressure + public signaling + the THREAT of standardized performance data, not from the scorecards themselves.

Why did this work? Because audit committees — empowered by Sarbanes-Oxley to oversee auditor selection — are a distinct stakeholder from management (who might prefer a more accommodating auditor). The PCAOB scorecard data empowers committee members to ask, "Why did we hire a firm with a 46% deficiency rate?" That question disciplines the shopping behavior even though the company still technically "chooses" its auditor.

In credit ratings, by contrast, ESMA has published comparable performance data (CEREP: transition matrices, default rates, per-agency comparison) since 2011. Yet issuer-pays rating shopping persists because **investors** — who would benefit from the data — don't control the assignment. The Franken Amendment (Section 939F, Dodd-Frank) proposed random assignment precisely because the SEC concluded transparency alone wouldn't break the shopping incentive.

**The structural insight:** transparency is a necessary-but-insufficient condition. The scorecard's power depends entirely on whether it empowers someone who controls the assignment to use it.

### Six domains mapped

#### Case 1: Auditing — STRONGEST evidence for scorecards working

**[CITED]** PCAOB has published inspection reports and deficiency findings since 2004 under SOX. In November 2024, PCAOB adopted mandatory firm and engagement metrics disclosure (Form FM + amended Form AP), requiring standardized public reporting of partner/manager involvement, workload, experience, retention, internal monitoring results, and restatement history.

Source: PCAOB Release No. 2024-012, November 21, 2024.  
<https://pcaobus.org/oversight/standards/standard-setting-research-projects/firm-and-engagement-metrics>

**[CITED]** Aggregate Part I.A audit deficiency rate dropped from **46% (2023) to 39% (2024)** across all inspected firms. For the six U.S. Global Network Firms (Big Four + GT + BDO), the rate was 26% in 2024. KPMG dropped from 26% to 20%. The PCAOB attributed this to "focused efforts under this Board to encourage firms to address their high deficiency rates."

Source: PCAOB Spotlight, 2024 inspection results.  
<https://pcaobus.org/news-events/news-releases/news-release-detail/pcaob-posts-report-detailing-significant-improvements-across-largest-firms--alongside-inspection-results-in-record-time>

**[CITED]** Over the 2009-2023 period, annually inspected firms had **consistently lower** deficiency rates than triennially inspected firms — inspection frequency (which increases visibility) correlates with better performance. Average deficiency rate across 15 years: 35.9%.

Source: CPA Journal, "Insights from Fifteen Years of PCAOB Inspections," January 2026.  
<https://www.cpajournal.com/2026/01/05/insights-from-fifteen-years-of-pcaob-inspections/>

**[CITED]** PCAOB experiments on auditor behavior find that mandatory partner rotation AND inspection jointly affect audit effort, with potential quality problems surfacing during transition periods — PCAOB inspection at higher rates during rotation years could mitigate this.

Source: Aaahq.org/AJPT, "The Joint Effects of Partner Rotation and PCAOB Inspections on Audit Effort."  
<https://publications.aaahq.org/ajpt/article/40/3/155/6195/The-Joint-Effects-of-Partner-Rotation-and-PCAOB>

**[INFERRED]** Why auditing is the strongest case: audit committees, empowered by SOX Section 301, are the legally designated body responsible for auditor selection and oversight. They are DISTINCT from management (who might prefer a less aggressive auditor). PCAOB performance data empowers audit committees to constrain management's shopping behavior. The triangulation works: PCAOB → audit committee → management.

#### Case 2: Credit ratings — WEAKEST case, transparency insufficient alone

**[CITED]** ESMA has operated CEREP (Central Repository for Credit Rating Activity and Performance Statistics) since 2011, publishing semi-annual data including transition matrices and default rates per registered CRA. The database allows direct comparison of agency accuracy.

Source: ESMA CEREP Publication.  
<https://registers.esma.europa.eu/cerep-publication/>

**[CITED]** Despite CEREP transparency, the credit rating market structure has barely changed. The Big Three (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) still dominate, and issuer-pays shopping remains structurally intact.

Source: PwC Legal, "Revisiting ESMA's 2023 CRA Market Share Report."  
<https://legal.pwc.de/en/news/articles/revisiting-esmas-2023-cra-market-share-report-what-now-in-2024-and-beyond>

**[CITED]** The Franken-Sherman Amendment (Section 939F of Dodd-Frank, 2010) proposed creating a Credit Rating Agency Board to independently assign NRSROs to rate structured finance products — essentially random/semi-random assignment. The SEC studied it and recommended against implementation in its December 2012 report, but the fact that full structural reform was seriously considered underscores that transparency alone was judged insufficient.

Source: Chapman & Cutler, "Franken Amendment (Section 939F)."  
<https://www.chapman.com/insights-sfi-9>

**[INFERRED]** Why credit ratings fail the scorecard test: the party who would benefit from performance data (investors) doesn't control the assignment. Issuers choose the CRA. CEREP data exists but investors can't force issuers to use better-calibrated agencies. The triangulation breaks: there is no empowered intermediary between the scorecard audience (investors) and the assignment decision (issuers). Q114's finding that assignment control is the proximal lever holds — transparency without leverage over assignment is decorative.

#### Case 3: Healthcare — MIXED evidence, illustrates Goodhart's Law tension

**[CITED]** CMS Hospital Quality Star Ratings assign 1-5 stars to hospitals using 45+ quality measures across five domains (Mortality, Safety, Readmission, Patient Experience, Timely/Effective Care). In 2026, CMS finalized changes making patient safety **foundational** — safety now defines the ceiling of the overall rating.

Source: Press Ganey, "CMS is changing how patient safety measures affect hospital Star Ratings."  
<https://www.pressganey.com/resources/blog/cms-is-changing-how-patient-safety-measures-affect-hospital-star-ratings/>

**[CITED]** Werner & Asch (JAMA 2005) documented unintended consequences of public quality reporting: physicians avoiding sick patients to improve rankings, target-rate gaming, discounting patient preferences. "Public reporting may inadvertently reduce, rather than improve, quality."

Source: Werner RM, Asch DA. "The unintended consequences of publicly reporting quality information." JAMA 2005;293(10):1239-44.  
<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15755946/>

**[CITED]** CMS star ratings have continued trending downward despite hospital quality improvement efforts since the pandemic — suggesting the metrics are resistant to pure gaming (or that gaming doesn't produce genuine improvement).

Source: Advisory Board, "CMS' latest Star Ratings are out."  
<https://www.advisory.com/daily-briefing/2025/08/13/cms-star-ratings>

**[INFERRED]** Healthcare star ratings partially work because patients and referring physicians can use them for hospital selection (the audience has SOME choice power). But the gaming problem persists because:
(a) Metrics are process-based rather than outcome-based for many dimensions, making them gameable.
(b) Patient capacity to use complex quality data is limited — most patients choose hospitals based on proximity and insurance network, not star ratings.
(c) The 2026 safety-as-ceiling reform shows CMS iterating to counter gaming — suggesting the system learns but never fully wins.

#### Case 4: Organic certification — shopping persists despite accreditation audits

**[CITED]** USDA NOP accredits 80+ organic certifiers and conducts enforcement activities. The Strengthening Organic Enforcement (SOE) rule updated regulations to improve oversight, including NOP 4002 enforcement penalty matrix.

Source: USDA AMS, "Organic Enforcement."  
<https://www.ams.usda.gov/services/enforcement/organic>

**[INFERRED]** Despite accreditation and public enforcement actions, certifier shopping remains possible because:
(a) Operations choose their certifier.
(b) NOP accreditation sets a floor but doesn't publish comparative performance scorecards (accuracy rates, noncompliance detection rates per certifier).
(c) No public mechanism makes "lenient certifiers" visible.

This is the scorecard-absent case: the accreditation structure exists but produces no public COMPARATIVE performance data. You can look up enforcement actions against specific certifiers, but there's no CEREP-equivalent for organic certification — no systematic "which certifier catches the most noncompliance?" data. The absence of public comparative data means the third party (consumers, retailers) cannot discipline shopping.

#### Case 5: Academic peer review — emerging transparency, structural lags

**[CITED]** EASE (European Association of Science Editors) has published guidance on assessing peer review quality, but no journal systematically publishes per-reviewer calibration data.

Source: EASE Peer Review Toolkit.  
<https://ease.org.uk/communities/peer-review-committee/peer-review-toolkit/how-to-assess-peer-review-quality/>

**[INFERRED]** Peer review has no public performance scorecard equivalent. Some journals internally track reviewer reliability (on-time rates, agreement with editorial decisions), but this data stays private. Bornmann et al.'s finding (Q114) that author-suggested reviewers are 30-42% more favorable than editor-selected reviewers is a RESEARCH finding, not a system feature — no journal publishes "reviewer X's historical accuracy" to authors or readers. The shopping mechanism (author-suggested reviewers) remains unconstrained by transparency because no transparency exists.

#### Case 6: Mandatory audit firm rotation — structural alternative that scorecards might approximate

**[CITED]** Italy has had mandatory audit firm rotation for 20+ years. Cameran et al. (Bocconi) find that auditors become MORE conservative in the 3-year period preceding mandatory rotation — firms invest in final-year quality because the rotation deadline is public and known.

Source: Cameran, Prencipe & Trombetta, "Mandatory Audit Firm Rotation and Audit Quality: Evidence from the Italian Setting."  
<https://accounting.unibocconi.eu/sites/default/files/files/media/attachments/paper_seminar%2520Bocconi%2520PRENCIPE20130605143857.pdf>

**[CITED]** A structured literature review of 128 empirical studies on MAR (2000-2022) finds that "assessing whether MAR benefits audit quality remains challenging because the research outcomes depend on the research question, rotation type, research method, and regulations enforced."

Source: ScienceDirect, "A structured literature review of empirical research on mandatory auditor rotation."  
<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1061951824000296>

**[INFERRED]** Mandatory rotation is the structural equivalent of random assignment: it breaks the long-term relationship that enables shopping. The Italian evidence suggests it works at the margin (increased conservatism before rotation) but at a cost (loss of client-specific knowledge). Scorecards may achieve some of the same deterrence WITHOUT the knowledge-loss cost — if the governance conditions hold.

### The three-condition model

Synthesizing across all six domains:

#### Condition 1: Scorecard audience has leverage over the assignment

| Domain | Audience | Leverage? | Shopping neutralized? |
|--------|----------|-----------|----------------------|
| Auditing | Audit committees | YES (SOX 301) | Partially ✓ |
| Credit ratings | Investors | NO (issuers assign) | No ✗ |
| Healthcare | Patients | WEAK (proximity/insurance dominate) | Weakly ✓ |
| Organic | Consumers/retailers | WEAK (no comparative data) | No ✗ |
| Peer review | Editors | YES (editor controls assignment when they choose to) | Partially ✓ (when editors use their power) |
| Audit rotation | N/A | STRUCTURAL (rotation mandated) | Yes ✓ |

#### Condition 2: Metric harder to game than the behavior it measures

| Domain | Primary metric | Gameable? |
|--------|---------------|-----------|
| Auditing | Work paper review by inspectors | HARD to game (examines actual procedures) |
| Credit ratings | Default rates, transition matrices | MODERATE (ratings are their own output; gaming IS the behavior) |
| Healthcare | Process + outcome measures | MODERATE-HIGH (documentation gaming, patient selection) |
| Organic | On-site inspections | MODERATE (inspection timing predictable) |

#### Condition 3: Credible enforcement backstop

| Domain | Enforcer | Credible? |
|--------|----------|-----------|
| Auditing | PCAOB (sanctions, deregistration) | YES |
| Credit ratings | SEC/ESMA | MODERATE (fines exist but rare) |
| Healthcare | CMS (payment adjustments, star ratings) | YES (financial consequences) |
| Organic | NOP (revocation of accreditation) | MODERATE (enforcement criticized as slow) |

**[INFERRED]** The strongest cases for scorecard-as-substitute (auditing, healthcare with caveats) have all three conditions present. The weakest cases (credit ratings, organic) have at most one. The pattern is consistent.

### Goodhart's Law as boundary condition

The central tension: scorecards work by making poor performance visible, but Goodhart's Law predicts that once a metric becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

**[CITED]** Werner & Asch (2005) documented this precisely in healthcare: public cardiac surgery mortality rates caused surgeons to avoid high-risk patients — the metric improved but the underlying behavior worsened.

**[INFERRED]** However, the PCAOB case suggests a partial antidote to Goodhart's Law: **process-based inspections that examine HOW work was done, not just WHAT the outcome was.** PCAOB inspectors review actual audit work papers, not just whether the audit opinion was "correct" in hindsight. This makes the metric much harder to game because you can't optimize a process that's evaluated by expert human review of the full work product.

**[INFERRED]** This generates a design principle: scorecards resist Goodhart's Law to the extent that they measure PROCESS quality (inspectable by experts) rather than OUTCOME metrics (optimizable by the evaluated party). Process audits are expensive but robust. Outcome metrics are cheap but gameable.

The Goodhart resistance spectrum:
- **Most resistant:** Expert process review (PCAOB inspection of work papers)
- **Moderately resistant:** Outcome metrics with risk adjustment (CMS mortality with case-mix adjustment)
- **Least resistant:** Pure output metrics without process visibility (credit rating accuracy, which is what the agency chose to give)

### The triangulation mechanism — why this matters for M3

Q114 established that assignment control is often the proximal lever for curator independence. Q119 now adds: **public performance data is the best available substitute for structural assignment reform, but it works through a specific governance mechanism — triangulation — rather than through direct disciplinary pressure on the evaluator.**

The mechanism:
1. Regulator/researcher **creates** the scorecard
2. An **empowered third party** (audit committee, informed consumer, editor) **uses** it to constrain...
3. The **assigning party** (management, issuer, author) from shopping for soft evaluators

If step 2 is missing — if nobody with assignment leverage sees or acts on the scorecard — the data becomes decorative. ESMA publishes CEREP. Nobody with assignment power uses it. The shopping continues.

This connects directly to the M3 chain: in curator markets where random assignment is impractical (most real-world cases), the design question is not "what metric?" but "who can act on the metric?" The audience for the scorecard IS the design variable.

Q112 showed curator markets usually optimize for balanced legitimacy.
Q113 showed the strongest discernment structures decouple both funding and assignment from the rated party.
Q114 showed assignment control is often the more proximal lever.
Q119 now shows: **transparency can partially substitute for assignment control, but only when it empowers someone who influences the assignment to use the performance data.** This is the middle path between full random assignment (impractical in most domains) and no structural reform (insufficient). The practical reform vector is not "publish more data" but "identify who in the governance structure can constrain shopping, and arm them with comparative performance data."

### Research patterns

#### Triangulation-as-governance (NEW)
When a public performance scorecard is created, the design question is not "what metric?" but "who can act on the metric?" Scorecards work through triangulation: A creates data, B uses it to constrain C. If B (the audience with leverage) is missing or powerless, the data is decorative. The audience IS the design variable.

#### Process-audit-as-Goodhart-resistance (NEW)
Scorecards resist Goodhart's Law to the extent that they measure expert-reviewable process rather than optimizable outcomes. PCAOB inspections examine HOW work was done (work papers), not just WHAT the result was (opinion accuracy). This makes gaming much harder because the metric is a high-dimensional expert judgment, not a reducible number. Wherever possible, design scorecard metrics as process inspections rather than outcome tallies.

#### Partial-substitution pricing
The question "can transparency substitute for structural reform?" doesn't have a binary answer. It has a conditional answer: yes, to about 60-80% effectiveness, when the three conditions hold. The residual 20-40% is the irreducible shopping margin that only structural reform (rotation, random assignment, regulatory exclusion) can close. Knowing the substitution rate helps policymakers choose between the cost of structural reform and the cost of residual shopping.
