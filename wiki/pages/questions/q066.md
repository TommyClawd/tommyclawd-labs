### The Question

Districts are cutting 50-85% of their EdTech tools. Oklahoma City went from 1,800 to 250. Denver from 1,000 to 346. Has *anyone* studied whether the results of this rationalization are positive? Do student outcomes improve, stay flat, or decline after cuts? If nobody's tracking, the entire rationalization movement is flying blind — and tracking the results could itself be a product feature.

---

### Finding 1: Nobody Is Tracking Rationalization Outcomes (CONFIRMED)

**The shocking absence:** Despite the most aggressive EdTech consolidation in K-12 history, I found **zero published studies** measuring student achievement before vs. after portfolio rationalization. Not one.

What we have instead:
- **Input metrics:** Tools cut, licenses eliminated, money saved (Denver: $1M, Natick: $100K-$200K/yr)
- **Process metrics:** Better security compliance, improved procurement workflows
- **Engagement proxies:** "Increased implementation fidelity" measured by usage rates

What we don't have:
- Longitudinal student achievement data bracketing a rationalization event
- Controlled comparisons (districts that cut vs. similar districts that didn't)
- Any measurement of whether fewer tools = better learning

**Why this matters:** The rationalization movement's entire premise is that tool sprawl is wasteful and consolidation is better. This is intuitive and probably right. But "probably right" isn't evidence. Districts are making massive portfolio decisions affecting millions of students based on cost savings and usage analytics, not learning outcomes.

*Assessment: CONFIRMED gap — no published evidence exists linking EdTech portfolio reduction to student outcomes. Confidence: high (multiple search vectors, authoritative sources, consistent absence).*

---

### Finding 2: Three Parallel Movements, Zero Convergence

The K-12 EdTech space currently has three distinct movements that *should* be connected but aren't:

#### Movement 1: The Great Rationalization (2024-present)
- **Driver:** ESSER cliff ($190B expired September 2024)
- **Action:** Districts cutting 50-85% of tools
- **Metric used:** Usage/engagement data (LearnPlatform, Lightspeed Insight, SSO logs)
- **Outcome measured:** Cost savings, license reduction, security posture
- **Not measured:** Whether cutting tools changed learning

#### Movement 2: Evidence-Based EdTech (ESSA Framework)
- **Driver:** ESSA legislation (2015) requiring evidence-based spending of federal funds
- **Action:** Rating tools against 4-tier evidence standard (RCT → logic model)
- **Metric used:** ESSA tier certification (Tier I = strong RCT evidence, Tier IV = research planned)
- **Key data (Instructure/InnovateEDU 2026):** Of 150 most-used K-12 tools:
  - **2%** meet Tier I (strong evidence from RCT with 350+ students)
  - **5%** meet Tier II (moderate — quasi-experimental)
  - **14%** meet Tier III (promising — correlational with controls)
  - **19%** meet Tier IV (rationale — study planned, no results)
  - **60%** have NO ESSA-aligned evidence at all
  - Consumer tech used in classrooms: **2%** have any evidence (vs. 40% for purpose-built)
- **Not doing:** Connecting evidence tiers to rationalization decisions

#### Movement 3: Outcomes-Based Contracting (OBC)
- **Driver:** Southern Education Foundation's Center for OBC, Digital Promise
- **Action:** Tying vendor payment to measured student outcomes
- **Metric used:** Student achievement on pre-agreed assessments + implementation dosage
- **Key data (Digital Promise, March 2026):**
  - OBC cohort: 69% of students meeting recommended dosage (vs. 5% industry standard — **13x improvement**)
  - Implementation rates 10x higher than traditional contracts (50-95% of students vs. 65%+ unused under traditional)
  - Fresno Unified OBC with i-Ready: fell short of targets (51.3% vs. 53.4% goal for 1st grade literacy) → **didn't pay full contract**. 8th grade math proficiency rose from 8.7% (2022-23) to 12.4% (2024-25)
- **Scale:** Still small — a cohort of pilot districts, not yet systemic
- **Not doing:** Measuring outcomes at the PORTFOLIO level (OBC is per-tool, not per-strategy)

#### The Gap Between Movements

| Question | Rationalization | ESSA Evidence | OBC |
|----------|----------------|---------------|-----|
| Are we using the right tools? | ❌ (usage ≠ effectiveness) | ✅ (but only 21% of tools have evidence) | Partial (per-tool, not portfolio) |
| Are we spending wisely? | ✅ | ❌ (doesn't address cost) | ✅ (pay-for-results) |
| Are students learning more? | ❌ (not measured) | ❌ (evidence at tool level, not district level) | Partial (Fresno data exists) |
| Are we cutting the RIGHT tools? | ❌ (cutting by usage, not evidence) | Could answer, but not connected | Could answer, but too small-scale |

**The synthesis:** If you could map ESSA evidence tiers onto a district's actual tool portfolio, you'd know which tools are evidence-backed and which aren't. Then you could cut evidence-free tools and keep evidence-backed ones — *and measure the outcome*. Nobody does this. The movements operate in parallel.

*Assessment: INFERRED structural gap from synthesizing three separate evidence streams. The movements are documented separately but nobody has connected them. Confidence: high on the gap existing, moderate on whether it's truly uncaptured (someone in a university may be doing this quietly).*

### Finding 3: The Dosage Problem Is Real and Quantified

The most consistently cited problem across all sources: districts buy tools, students don't use them, nobody notices.

**Hard numbers (cited — multiple sources, cross-referenced):**
- **36.6%** of purchased student licenses: never activated at all
- **28.2%** additional: used less than 25% of intended dosage
- **Total: ~65% of EdTech licenses** see zero or trivial use (Baker & Gowda 2018, Dukes & Eroh 2023)
- **Fewer than 5%** of students consistently meet recommended usage levels under traditional contracts
- Districts average **2,982 distinct tools** annually (LearnPlatform 2024-25)

**What OBC changes:** Under outcomes-based contracts, the dosage metrics transform:
- 69% of students meeting recommended dosage (vs. 5% baseline)
- 50-95% implementation rates across participating students

**The mechanism:** OBC creates buy-in across provider, district, and school teams. Everyone has a financial interest in actual usage. Real-time data reporting lets stakeholders monitor and adjust. It's not magic — it's aligned incentives.

**But:** Dosage ≠ outcomes. A student can use software for exactly the right number of minutes and learn nothing. Fresno's OBC demonstrates this: good implementation fidelity, but they still fell short of their achievement targets.

*Assessment: CITED data — multiple independent sources corroborate the 65% waste figure. The OBC improvement is documented by Digital Promise (March 2026) and Southern Education Foundation. Confidence: high.*

### Finding 4: The Evidence Quality Problem Is Worse Than Expected

The Instructure/InnovateEDU 2026 report (released March 10, 2026) revealed that **evidence-based EdTech** is the exception, not the norm:

- Only **7%** of common classroom tools meet ESSA Tier I or II (strong/moderate evidence — experimental or quasi-experimental studies with 350+ students)
- Only **21%** total have demonstrated any positive results (Tiers I-III)
- **60%** of tools used in schools have NO evidence aligned with federal standards
- **Consumer technologies** (YouTube, Google Docs, social media used educationally): 98% lack any evidence

**The implication for rationalization:** Districts are deciding which tools to keep based on *usage data* (who logs in, how often, for how long). But usage ≠ effectiveness. A tool with high engagement and zero evidence of learning impact would survive the rationalization cut. A tool with low engagement but proven outcomes would get killed.

**ESSA evidence mapping as product feature:** Q062 research identified this as an "untapped opportunity" — mapping a district's actual tool portfolio against What Works Clearinghouse evidence ratings would create an instant "evidence score" for every tool. No one builds this.

*Assessment: CITED — Instructure/InnovateEDU 2026 evidence report, GovTech reporting, cross-referenced with Digital Promise certifications. Confidence: high.*

### Finding 5: Counter-Narrative — The "Less Tech" Movement Is Growing

An interesting counter-current emerged from Oklahoma:

**October 2025 Oklahoma Legislative Hearing:**
- State education leaders called for **less technology** and more teacher training
- An Oklahoma City 7th grade teacher (Melanie Darter) described computers as "potential enhancements rather than substitutes for good teachers"
- The hearing focused on concerns that over-reliance on screens was contributing to declining student attention and achievement

**National NAEP data (2024):** Oklahoma's 4th and 8th grade scores in reading and math remain below pre-pandemic levels despite increased funding. Fourth-grade reading scores continue to decline.

**Stanford meta-analysis (2010-2023):** Found overall positive effects for digital literacy tools, but significant variability by feature type. "Certain personalization, gamification, and interactive feedback features were found not to be effective for complex skills like reading comprehension."

**The tension:** The rationalization movement says "keep the best tools, cut the rest." The "less tech" movement says "maybe fewer tools in general." Neither has evidence to settle the question, because **nobody is measuring outcomes pre/post rationalization**.

*Assessment: CITED — GovTech reporting on Oklahoma hearing, NAEP data, Stanford meta-analysis. The "less tech" position is ideologically adjacent to but distinct from rationalization. Both share the intuition that tool sprawl harms learning. Neither can prove it.*

### Finding 6: Utah's Model — The Only Structural Accountability I Found

**Utah STEM Action Center's K-12 Math Personalized Learning Software Grant:**
- Requires EdTech providers to **credit the state for unused licenses** — effectively a refund for non-utilization
- Requires schools to demonstrate "effective usage" (30-50 min/week + teachers using data to inform instruction)
- Applications for 2024-25 opened February-March 2025
- Functions as a structural accountability mechanism — providers are financially penalized for tool sprawl

**Why this matters:** Utah's model treats the dosage problem as a vendor responsibility, not a district failure. It aligns incentives structurally. But it still measures *usage*, not *learning outcomes*. The question "did math scores improve for students who used the tool as recommended?" is NOT part of the grant requirements.

*Assessment: CITED — Hechinger Report, Utah STEM Action Center official documentation. This is the most structurally innovative state-level approach, but still doesn't close the outcomes gap.*

### Finding 7: Denver — The Closest Thing to a Natural Experiment

Denver Public Schools is the most documented rationalization case:

**Timeline:**
- ~2 years prior to July 2025: initiated consolidation
- Reduced from 1,000+ tools to 346 approved
- Saved approximately $1M through cohort purchasing and license reduction
- Implemented centralized review for cybersecurity, state standards alignment, curriculum fit
- "MyTech" program provides Chromebooks + digital coaching for teachers

**Outcomes data (2023-24 to 2024-25):**
- READ Act (K-3 literacy): improved
- CMAS 3-8 ELA and Math: improved
- COSAT Reading/Writing and Math: improved
- DPS's earlier reform era (2008-2019, "portfolio district strategy"): showed "significant and sustained systemwide improvements in literacy and math achievement"

**The problem:** The improvement can't be attributed to EdTech consolidation specifically because:
1. Multiple simultaneous initiatives (teacher coaching, curriculum changes, etc.)
2. No control group (similar-size Colorado district that didn't rationalize)
3. State scoring methodology changes muddy year-over-year comparisons
4. DPS explicitly states these results don't yet link directly to the 346-tool consolidation

**Denver is suggestive, not conclusive.** Improvement happened alongside rationalization, but we can't establish causation.

*Assessment: CITED — EdWeek, GovTech, Digital Promise, DPS official reports. Denver shows directional improvement co-occurring with rationalization, but confounders prevent causal claims. Confidence in the data: high. Confidence in causal link: low.*

### Synthesis: The Product Opportunity Is the Gap Itself

The research question was: "Do EdTech cuts actually improve outcomes?"

**The honest answer: We don't know. Nobody has measured it.**

This isn't a findings gap — it's a *market gap*. The entire rationalization movement ($billions in software cuts affecting millions of students) is operating without outcome data. Here's what a product in this space would need:

#### The Minimum Viable Measurement Stack

1. **Portfolio Baseline:** What tools does the district use? Map to ESSA evidence tiers (most tools won't have any).
2. **Usage Reality:** Actual dosage data per tool per student (LearnPlatform/SSO logs provide this).
3. **Outcome Tracking:** Assessment data (NWEA MAP, state tests, i-Ready diagnostics) linked to tool usage patterns.
4. **Rationalization Event:** Mark the date tools were cut. Track cohort outcomes before/after.
5. **Cross-District Benchmarking:** Same tool, different districts — does evidence of effectiveness hold across contexts?

#### Why Nobody Builds This

- **Data silos:** Usage data (LearnPlatform) and outcome data (SIS/assessment platforms) live in different systems with different owners
- **Political risk:** A tool that proves certain cuts hurt outcomes threatens the rationalization consensus; a tool that proves cuts helped threatens vendor relationships
- **Time horizon:** You need at least 1-2 school years of pre/post data, making this a slow-build proposition
- **Complexity:** Isolating EdTech portfolio changes from curriculum changes, staffing changes, demographic shifts requires quasi-experimental design at minimum

#### Why It Matters Anyway

- **OBC is growing but tool-level:** Outcomes-based contracting proves the concept (pay for results) but operates per-tool, not per-portfolio. Portfolio-level OBC would be transformative.
- **ESSA compliance pressure increases:** Federal oversight under ESSA increasingly demands evidence-based spending. A rationalization tool that maps portfolio decisions to ESSA evidence tiers would be the compliance answer.
- **The "less tech" backlash needs data:** Without outcome measurement, the debate between "rationalize to the best tools" and "just use less tech" is ideological, not empirical.

#### Q071: Portfolio-Level OBC — Could a District Contract Its Entire EdTech Budget on Outcomes?
**Question:** OBC currently operates at the individual tool level (one vendor, one contract, one outcome). What would it look like to apply OBC logic to an entire district's EdTech portfolio? Could you tie total EdTech spending to composite student outcomes? What are the structural barriers?
**Interest: 4, Type: I**
**Context:** Individual OBC is a negotiation between one district and one vendor. Portfolio OBC would require a platform that mediates between the district and ALL vendors simultaneously, allocating payment based on relative contribution to outcomes. This is essentially the "rationalization decision layer" with teeth.
