### Findings

#### The Short Answer: Yes, This Study Is Feasible and Would Be a First — But HackerOne Would Almost Certainly Resist

No one has published a direct solver-hours/dollar measurement for any major bounty platform. The study design is straightforward. The political economy of getting it done is the hard part.

---

#### 1. The Measurement Gap Is Real and Well-Documented

Multiple empirical studies have circled this number without measuring it:

- **Akgul et al. (USENIX Security 2023, n=159):** Collected self-reported weekly hours (34 hunters <5h/wk, 53 at 5-10h, 40 at 10-20h) and yearly income (<$1K for 33 participants, $1-30K for 57). But they never *divided* hours by dollars to get an hourly rate. The data exists in their survey responses — someone could reanalyze it — but the paper doesn't report the ratio.
- **Oxford/HackerOne study (Zhao et al., ORA):** Measured program-side economics extensively — $230/day average operating cost, $83,950/year average program cost, payout distributions by severity ($7,924 critical, $786 medium). But all metrics are buyer-side. Zero measurement of solver time input.
- **Ellis & Stevens, "Bounty Everything" (Data & Society/NSF, 2022):** Qualitative interviews with hackers about working conditions. Documents the piecework nature and insecurity. No quantitative time tracking.
- **Skavans (Medium, 2022):** One hunter's personal schedule — tried 8-10hr/day, burned out, settled on creative-work rhythms. Anecdotal. No systematic measurement.
- **Q146 triangulation:** Our prior research estimated ~40 solver-hours per $1,000 paid (optimistic), implying 2:1 to 10:1 effort-to-reward ratios depending on solver tier. But this was inference, not measurement.

**Bottom line:** Everyone describes the problem qualitatively. No one has instrumented it.

---

#### 2. Study Design: What Would Actually Work

A voluntary Toggl/RescueTime cohort study would need:

**Participants:** 100-200 active bug bounty hunters, stratified by:
- Experience tier (beginner/intermediate/elite, using HackerOne reputation score as proxy)
- Time commitment (part-time <10h/wk, serious 10-30h, full-time 30+)
- Geographic region (hourly opportunity cost varies dramatically: US/EU vs. India/Pakistan/Nigeria)

**Instrumentation:**
- **Toggl Track** (voluntary manual logging): Lower compliance but captures intent — hunter starts timer when working on a specific program. Categories: reconnaissance, testing, report writing, communication/triage, duplicate resolution, learning/skill-building.
- **RescueTime** (automated passive tracking): Higher compliance, captures actual screen time on security tools/platforms. But noisy — can't distinguish bounty work from other security research.
- **Hybrid is best:** RescueTime for total security-tool hours, Toggl for program-specific attribution.

**Duration:** 6-12 months minimum. Bug hunting has extreme variance — a hunter might spend 200 hours finding nothing, then earn $10K in a week. Short studies will capture misleading snapshots.

**Key metrics to produce:**
1. **Total solver-hours per dollar paid** (the headline number)
2. **Hours per accepted report** by severity
3. **Dead-end ratio:** Hours spent on work that produced $0 (no valid bug, duplicate, won't-fix)
4. **Effective hourly rate** by experience tier
5. **Opportunity cost ratio:** Effective rate vs. market rate for equivalent skills (pentesting/consulting benchmarks)

**Controls needed:**
- Distinguish platform time (HackerOne/Bugcrowd) from program time (testing the actual target)
- Capture learning/skill-building time that's bounty-adjacent but not program-specific
- Account for multi-tasking and context-switching (common among part-timers)

**IRB/Ethics:** Standard human subjects research. Self-selected sample bias is the main threat — hunters who agree to be tracked may be systematically different (more organized, higher earners) than those who don't. Acknowledged as limitation.

#### 3. Would HackerOne Cooperate or Resist?

**They would resist.** Here's why:

**Structural incentive against transparency:**
- HackerOne's value proposition to buyers is: "Pay only for results, get a global workforce testing your product for less than 2 engineers" (Oxford study confirms: avg program costs < 2 software engineer salaries)
- If the study reveals that hunters collectively spend 5-10x the payout value in labor, it reframes bounties from "efficient crowdsourced security" to "exploitative piecework" (Ellis & Stevens' framing)
- The platform *benefits* from overdissipation — more hunters competing = more bugs found per dollar. Quantifying waste undermines the pitch

**Specific resistance vectors:**
1. **Data access denial.** HackerOne won't share submission-level data (who submitted what, when, resolution time). The Oxford study used only public data from Hacktivity.
2. **Marketing capture.** HackerOne's own annual reports are explicitly "for marketing" and "not independently verified" (Akgul et al., 2023). They control the narrative.
3. **Hunter NDA/ToS constraints.** Many private programs have NDAs that could arguably restrict hunters from sharing time data about specific programs.
4. **Reputational risk.** If the median hunter earns $3-8/hr (plausible from Q146 triangulation), that's a PR problem in the post-gig-economy-backlash era.

**Potential cooperation pathways (lower probability):**
- **Academic partnership with credibility cover.** HackerOne has cooperated with researchers before (they didn't block Akgul et al., though they didn't help either). A top-tier university partnership (CMU, MIT, Oxford) gives them plausible deniability — "we support research."
- **Framing as efficiency improvement.** If the study is positioned as "helping platforms optimize bounty pricing to reduce hunter burnout and improve quality," HackerOne might see it as product research rather than exposé.
- **Hunter-side only study.** Bypass the platform entirely. Recruit hunters directly (as Akgul et al. did via Twitter/Reddit), have them self-track. No platform cooperation needed. This is the most likely path to execution.
- **Competing platform leverage.** Bugcrowd, Intigriti, or YesWeHack might cooperate precisely to differentiate from HackerOne. A smaller platform has less to lose and more to gain from transparency.

#### 4. What the Oxford Study Reveals About the Political Economy

The Oxford/HackerOne dataset (Zhao et al.) provides a crucial indirect data point:

- Average program: ~156 valid reports/year, $230/day operating cost
- Top 100 hackers: 147 reports each; top 5 users = 17.5% of all submissions
- **Weak correlation (R²=0.05) between bounty size and report volume** — meaning higher bounties don't proportionally attract more effort

This last finding is devastating for the platform narrative. If raising bounties doesn't increase discovery proportionally, it means the existing payout level already captures most available effort — hunters are already spending near-maximum time. The marginal solver-hour is essentially free to the platform.

Combined with the extreme skew (top 5 users = 17.5% of all bugs), the picture is: **a tiny elite earns well, a large base earns poverty wages, and the platform captures the difference as surplus.**

#### 5. Feasibility Assessment

| Factor | Assessment |
|--------|-----------|
| **Technical feasibility** | High — Toggl/RescueTime APIs exist, study design is standard |
| **Recruitment feasibility** | Medium — Akgul recruited 159 via Twitter/Reddit; 100-200 with time-tracking commitment is harder but doable with compensation ($50-100 gift cards) |
| **Platform cooperation** | Low (HackerOne), Medium (smaller platforms) |
| **Bypass feasibility** | High — hunter-side-only study doesn't need platform cooperation |
| **Publication venue** | Strong — USENIX Security, IEEE S&P, or economics journals would take this. Clean empirical contribution filling a documented gap |
| **Time to results** | 18-24 months (6mo design + 6-12mo data collection + 6mo analysis/writing) |
| **Funding** | NSF SaTC program is the natural fit — Ellis & Stevens got NSF funding (Award 1915815) for qualitative work; quantitative follow-up is a logical next grant |

#### 6. Why This Matters for M1

If education outcomes bounties follow bug bounty dynamics:
- The solver-hours/dollar ratio directly predicts whether outcome-based contracts attract enough solver effort to work
- If the ratio is 5:1+ (5 hours of solver labor per dollar paid), most solvers will exit, leaving only overconfident or intrinsically motivated participants — adverse selection
- **Design intervention:** The M1 product should build time-tracking INTO the platform from day one, making the ratio transparent and allowing dynamic bounty repricing
- This transparency feature would be a genuine competitive moat — no existing bounty platform does it
