**Date:** 2026-04-09  
**Status:** Researched  
**Question:** Across curator markets, is the decisive variable often not who funds the gate but who gets to choose it? In other words: if supplier shopping remains possible, does even a relatively clean funding model drift toward softness? Could random/blind assignment, anti-shopping rules, editor-controlled matching, or public performance ranking improve discernment more than changing the funding source alone?

---

### Bottom line

**Usually yes — but with an important refinement.**

**Funding source determines the direction of pressure. Evaluator assignment determines whether that pressure can be actively exploited through shopping.**

If I have to rank the two, the evidence from this pass suggests:

1. **Assignment is often the more proximal lever** — the one that most directly converts conflict into softer outcomes.
2. **Funding is the deeper background lever** — it sets the baseline incentive field, but often does not fully explain variation until you ask who gets to pick the evaluator.
3. **The strongest systems solve both.** Q113 remains intact: the best discernment architectures decouple **money** and **chooser rights** from the rated party.

So the cleanest answer is not:
- “funding doesn’t matter,” or
- “assignment always dominates.”

It is:

> **In many real curator markets, assignment control is the more decisive *margin of capture*, because chooser rights turn latent incentive problems into realized softness.**

The strongest evidence is where **funding is held roughly constant and only assignment changes**. In those settings, outcomes still move a lot.

---

### The deepest surprise

I expected this pass to end with a balanced “both matter.”

It still does, technically — but the most convincing evidence kept coming from cases where the funding model barely changed while the **selector** did:

- author-suggested vs. editor-selected peer reviewers,
- client switching / opinion shopping in auditing,
- issuer shopping across rating agencies,
- certifier shopping in accreditation systems,
- sponsor-shaped entry boundaries in prestige guides.

That means the cleanest evidence is not abstract theory about incentives. It is **selector-rights evidence**.

#### [INFERRED] Main update
The right question is not just *who pays the gate?*  
It is:

> **Who is allowed to route themselves to the gate most favorable to them?**

That looks like the more operational question.

---

### Working distinction: background incentive vs. realized capture

#### [INFERRED]
A useful distinction emerged across the cases:

- **Funding source** creates a **background incentive**.
  - Supplier-funded systems create a reason to be soft.
  - Audience-funded systems create a reason to satisfy audiences.
  - Public/nonprofit/endowment systems create different but still real incentive structures.

- **Assignment control** creates a **realized capture pathway**.
  - If the rated party can choose, switch, threaten to switch, or narrow the candidate set, it can push the gate toward accommodation.
  - If the rated party cannot choose, much of that pressure is blunted even if the evaluator still faces imperfect incentives.

This suggests a hierarchy:

> **Funding tells you where bias wants to go. Assignment tells you how much ability actors have to get there.**

---

### Case 1: Peer review — strongest clean evidence for assignment over funding

This was the sharpest evidence in the entire pass because the **funding structure is effectively held constant** while reviewer assignment changes.

#### [CITED] Author-suggested reviewers are systematically more favorable than editor-selected reviewers
Bornmann et al. studied 552 manuscripts / 1,145 reviews at *Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics* and found that:

> “editor-suggested reviewers rated manuscripts between **30% and 42% less favorably** than author-suggested reviewers.”

They explicitly controlled for manuscript quality.

Source: Bornmann et al., *Do Author-Suggested Reviewers Rate Submissions More Favorably than Editor-Suggested Reviewers?*  
<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2954795/>

#### [CITED] This pattern held even in public peer review
The same paper emphasizes that ACP used **interactive public peer review**, where reviews and replies were publicly exchanged. The openness did **not** remove the bias.

Source: same as above.

#### [CITED] Prior studies across multiple journals found the same directional result
Bornmann et al. review multiple earlier studies showing author-suggested reviewers tend to give more favorable recommendations than editor-selected reviewers across several journals and even grant review contexts.

Source: same as above.

#### [INFERRED] Why this case matters so much
This is the closest thing in this pass to a natural identification of the assignment effect.

- Same broad institutional context
- Same journal
- Same review system
- Same basic evaluator role
- No major funding-model change
- **Only assignment source differs materially**

Yet the evaluations move substantially.

#### [INFERRED] Implication
If holding funding essentially constant while changing selector rights produces a 30–42% favorability gap, then **assignment is not a secondary implementation detail**. It is a first-order independence variable.

This is the cleanest evidence I found that assignment can matter *more* than funding in practice, because it can be isolated more cleanly.

### Case 2: Auditing — chooser rights create successful opinion shopping

Auditing reinforces the same conclusion from a different domain.

#### [CITED] Post-SOX U.S. firms still successfully shop for cleaner audit opinions
A 2024 study on U.S. public firms after going-concern opinions found:

> “public companies in the U.S. who receive GCOs are **successful in shopping for clean audit opinions** in a subsequent period.”

It also found that this was more common when firms switched to **non-Big 4** auditors.

Source: *Do publicly traded companies engage in opinion shopping after receiving a going concern audit opinion? Evidence from U.S. firms in the post-SOX era*  
<https://scholars.nova.edu/en/publications/do-publicly-traded-companies-engage-in-opinion-shopping-after-rec/>

#### [CITED] Prior opinion-shopping literature finds the same structure
Search results and linked abstracts from Lennox-style frameworks and later work consistently describe successful opinion shopping or partner-level opinion shopping when clients can switch evaluators.

Examples surfaced in this pass:
- Lennox framework references on opinion shopping in UK audit markets
- partner-level opinion shopping evidence summarized on IDEAS/RePEc
- post-SOX distressed-firm opinion-shopping studies

Representative source surfaced in search:  
<https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/joares/v54y2016i1p79-112.html>

#### [INFERRED] Why this matters
The audit case is not just “funding matters because the client pays the auditor.” That was already known.

The sharper point is:

> **Client payment becomes most distortionary when combined with client choice and exit options.**

The threat is not only “my customer funds me.” It is:
- “my customer can fire me,”
- “my customer can seek a softer successor,”
- “future clients observe whether I am commercially difficult.”

#### [INFERRED] Implication
This makes assignment/retention design a more proximate lever than funding source alone.  
Even if we cannot abolish client-paid audits, reforms like:
- mandatory rotation,
- partner rotation,
- tighter dismissal scrutiny,
- stronger audit committee control,
- anti-shopping disclosure,
can still improve independence by constraining chooser power.

### Case 3: Credit ratings — the core distortion is not just issuer-pay, but issuer shopping

This domain produced the clearest interaction between the two axes.

#### [CITED] Increased issuer-paid competition worsened ratings quality
Becker & Milbourn found that Fitch’s greater competitive presence coincided with:
- **higher rating levels**,
- **weaker correlation between ratings and market-implied yields**, and
- **worse default prediction**.

Their abstract states:

> “increased competition from Fitch coincides with **lower quality ratings** from the incumbents.”

Source: Becker & Milbourn, *How did increased competition affect credit ratings?*  
<https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v101y2011i3p493-514.html>

#### [CITED] Brookings explicitly frames the issuer-pay problem through shopping
Brookings’ review says the issuer-pay model created a built-in conflict because issuers were **free to shop for a more accommodating CRA**.

Source: Brookings, *Credit rating agency reform is incomplete*  
<https://www.brookings.edu/articles/credit-rating-agency-reform-is-incomplete/>

#### [CITED] Dodd-Frank / Franken treated assignment as a distinct reform lever
A summary of the SEC’s assigned-ratings report notes that Section 939F required analysis of an **assignment system** for rating agencies, alongside other options like compensation changes.

Source: Orrick / JD Supra summary of SEC report  
<https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/sec-report-on-assigned-credit-ratings-50033/>

#### [CITED] Investor-paid entry sharpened issuer-paid S&P’s ratings
Han’s JFE paper found that when investor-paid Egan-Jones initiated coverage, S&P’s issuer-paid ratings became more responsive to credit risk and more informative.

Source: Han, *Can investor-paid credit rating agencies improve the information quality of issuer-paid rating agencies?*  
<https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v111y2014i2p450-468.html>

#### [INFERRED] What this case actually says
The credit-ratings literature does **not** support a naive “competition solves bias” story. In issuer-paid markets, more choice can worsen quality because it intensifies shopping pressure.

That means:
- funding matters (issuer-pay created the original conflict), **but**
- assignment/chooser rights determine how that conflict is exploited.

#### [INFERRED] Key refinement
Han’s result is especially informative because it shows that **changing the ecology of evaluator assignment/competition** can improve quality even without abolishing the issuer-paid incumbent model.

That suggests assignment structure may often be the **higher-leverage marginal reform**, even when funding remains imperfect.

### Case 4: USDA Organic and Michelin — capture often hides at the boundary/assignment margin

These cases matter because they show that formal non-payment at the scoring margin does **not** eliminate chooser influence.

#### [CITED] USDA Organic allows producers to choose among accredited certifiers
USDA explains that most accredited certifying agents can certify broadly and that producers may choose whom to work with.

Source: USDA AMS certifying-agents resources surfaced in search / Q113 pass  
<https://www.ams.usda.gov/resources/organic-certifying-agents>

#### [INFERRED]
That means public standards and accreditation can raise the floor, but **shopping risk persists** if operations can still select among certifiers.

#### [CITED] Michelin guide expansion has been sponsor-funded in some regions
Eater documented tourism-board/government funding helping bring Michelin guides into specific cities/regions.

Source: Eater, *The High Price of a Michelin Guide*  
<https://www.eater.com/2018/7/18/17540672/michelin-guides-restaurants-tourism-bangkok-thailand-south-korea-singapore-funding>

#### [INFERRED]
Michelin’s restaurant-star process may remain relatively insulated **within** a covered geography, yet sponsor money can shape **which geographies enter the evaluation universe at all**.

So even when restaurants do not literally buy stars, chooser/funder influence can still appear at the **boundary-setting margin**.

#### [INFERRED] Implication
This matters because it generalizes the assignment idea:

> Capture is not only “who grades me?” It can also be “who gets included, when, where, and by whom?”

Assignment is therefore broader than reviewer matching. It includes:
- inclusion/exclusion of cases,
- region/market entry,
- certifier choice,
- timing of evaluation,
- whether a second evaluator is invited.

---

### Why assignment often beats funding as a reform lever

#### [INFERRED]
Across these cases, assignment seems to beat funding in three ways.

#### 1. It is more proximal
Funding creates a motive; assignment creates the route.

If a system has biasing incentives but actors cannot choose or threaten evaluators, some capture pressure remains latent.
If actors can choose or threaten evaluators, the pressure becomes concrete.

#### 2. It is more identifiable
Peer review gave the clearest evidence exactly because selector rights changed while the institutional setting stayed mostly fixed.

By contrast, funding-model comparisons are often confounded by:
- domain differences,
- audience differences,
- platform differences,
- information asymmetries,
- and mission differences.

#### 3. It is often more reformable
Changing an entire business model can be politically and operationally hard.

Changing chooser rights may be easier:
- blind assignment,
- editor-controlled reviewer selection,
- random assignment from a qualified pool,
- anti-shopping rules,
- mandatory disclosure of rejections/preliminary ratings,
- rotation,
- committee oversight for switching.

#### [INFERRED] Reform hierarchy
If a policymaker asks “what can we change first that may actually matter?” the answer is often:

> **Constrain shopping and chooser rights before assuming a total funding-model rewrite is the only serious option.**

---

### But assignment is not everything

This pass did **not** support the claim that assignment universally dominates funding.

#### [INFERRED] Why funding still matters
If every evaluator in the pool shares the same soft incentive, random assignment among them may reduce shopping without fully eliminating the background bias.

Examples:
- If all certifiers are still heavily dependent on supplier fees,
- if all auditors fear commercial retaliation,
- if all subscriber-funded critics are audience-captured,
then assignment reform helps, but may not fully fix the epistemic problem.

#### [INFERRED] Cleanest formulation
So the better statement is:

> **Assignment is often more important at the margin; funding remains more important in the limit.**

Or more plainly:
- **Assignment often explains the observed softness.**
- **Funding often explains why the system remains soft even after assignment reform.**

That seems to be the best synthesis.

---

### Direct answer to Q114

### Is evaluator assignment more important than funding source for curator independence?

**Usually yes, in the practical sense that assignment is often the more decisive and observable margin of capture.**

The strongest evidence from this pass shows:

1. **Holding funding roughly constant while changing assignment changes outcomes a lot** (peer review).
2. **Client choice / exit options enable softer outcomes even under existing regulation** (auditing).
3. **Competition worsens quality when chooser rights intensify shopping** (credit ratings under issuer-pay).
4. **Boundary-setting influence survives even where direct payment-for-score is absent** (Michelin, USDA-style certifier choice).

#### [INFERRED] Best formulation
> **If funding source tells you where bias points, assignment tells you whether actors can weaponize that bias. In many curator markets, chooser rights are therefore the more important practical independence variable.**

#### [INFERRED] Strongest policy implication
The best reforms are usually not “change who pays” *instead of* “change who picks.”  
They are:
- **reduce chooser control,**
- **reduce shopping,**
- **publish performance data,**
- **separate standards from commercial operations,**
- and when possible, **also improve funding independence.**

That is the real answer.

---

### Confidence map

#### High confidence
- Peer review provides strong evidence that assignment source alone materially affects evaluator softness.
- Audit opinion shopping shows chooser rights remain consequential even in regulated settings.
- Credit-ratings evidence strongly supports the view that issuer shopping is a core transmission mechanism of bias.
- Michelin/USDA reinforce that capture can persist through boundary or certifier choice even without crude pay-for-score structures.

#### Medium confidence
- Assignment is usually the more important **marginal reform lever**.
- Public performance ranking / anti-shopping disclosure may substitute for full assignment control in some domains.
- The selector-rights framework may travel across many curator markets, not just the cases studied here.

#### Unknown
- Whether assignment dominates funding in all domains or mainly in multi-evaluator / shopping-prone markets.
- How much random assignment harms specialization/matching quality in domains needing expertise fit.
- Whether public performance scorecards can neutralize shopping without banning evaluator choice.
