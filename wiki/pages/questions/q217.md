### Findings

### Bottom line
The artifact most likely to convert evidence into procurement points is **not the efficacy study itself**; it is a short **ESSA-tier memo**, preferably backed by a recognized third-party **certification/badge**, plus an **implementation/dosage package** when the rubric scores feasibility or renewal risk.

**Cited:** ESSA tiers are the policy language buyers are being given; the U.S. Department of Education/OET released guidance and one-pagers breaking down evidence levels amid demand for edtech research. Tier 4 requires explaining strategies, grounding them in research, and showing why design should improve outcomes. Digital Promise’s ESSA Tier 3 certification verifies a learning-sciences basis and at least one well-designed study aligned to ESSA Tier 3. Leanlab notes districts are evaluating long-term procurement and want tools that work with evidence behind claims.

**Inference:** Raw efficacy studies have high proof value but lower procurement-conversion unless translated into evaluator language: claim, population, study design, outcome, ESSA tier, implementation conditions, and replication requirements. The memo/badge reduces evaluator work and maps to RFP checkboxes; the study supplies the substrate.

#### Reliability ranking for pre-award scoring
1. **ESSA tier memo** — highest: direct mapping to statutory/procurement evidence language.
2. **Third-party certification/badge** — high: quick trust signal if criteria are transparent.
3. **Implementation plan** — high when rubrics score feasibility, PD, rostering, data/privacy, or change management.
4. **Efficacy study** — medium-high: strongest underlying proof, but weaker as a procurement artifact unless summarized.
5. **References** — medium: de-risks adoption and local fit, but usually anecdotal.
6. **Dosage/fidelity dashboard** — medium pre-award, high for pilots/renewals: proves usage can be monitored and outcomes attributed.

**Unknown:** The supplied sources do not provide empirical win-rate or point-conversion data by artifact. Treat the ranking as a procurement-design inference, not a measured fact.
