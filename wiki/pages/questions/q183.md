### Q183: PLG "Moving Upmarket" — Price Raises vs. Genuine Enterprise Acquisition

#### The Uncomfortable Answer: No One Publishes This Breakdown

There is **no published study** that directly measures what percentage of PLG companies claiming to "move upmarket" are genuinely acquiring new enterprise logos versus simply raising prices on their existing SMB/mid-market base. This is a measurement gap — and likely an intentional one, because the answer is unflattering for many companies.

However, converging indirect evidence paints a clear picture:

#### What the Data Actually Shows

**1. Expansion revenue dominates "upmarket" ARR growth.**
- At high-performing SaaS companies, expansion revenue now accounts for **40–50% of new ARR** (High Alpha analysis, cited by SaaS Mag 2026). This is revenue from *existing* customers — upsells, seat growth, pricing tier upgrades — not new enterprise logos.
- Iconiq Capital's multi-year ARR mix analysis showed that during the 2021–2023 downturn, the mix shifted **>10 percentage points** toward expansion (existing base) vs. new logos, as companies leaned on what they already had.
- Acquiring a new customer costs **5–7x more** than expanding an existing one. The rational economic move is to extract more from the base first.

**2. NRR is the mechanism — and it conflates both strategies.**
- Median NRR for public SaaS: **107%** (45/110 disclosing). Best-in-class enterprise: **120%+**.
- A company can report 120% NRR — which looks like "moving upmarket" — entirely through seat expansion, usage-based pricing increases, and feature-gating existing users into higher tiers. No new enterprise logo required.
- SaaS Mag (2026): "A PLG company with 90% NRR is just a cheaper acquisition channel. A PLG company with 130%+ NRR is a compounding machine." But that compounding can come from **pricing pressure on the existing base**, not enterprise penetration.

**3. The "enterprise tier" is often a price gate, not a market shift.**
- Pocus's PLG benchmarks note companies commonly create "weak differentiation in their enterprise offering" — adding SSO, audit logs, and compliance features as justification for **3x pricing** on functionally similar products. The Pocus framing is explicit: this is packaging theater for existing users, not genuine enterprise go-to-market.
- HelloAdvisr (pricing consultancy): "If you think enterprise buyers 'just pay more,' you will miss the gaps between value and packaging." Many PLG companies are doing exactly this — adding a coat of paint to justify higher ACVs.

**4. Figma is the rare genuine case — and even there, the pattern is nuanced.**
- Figma crossed $1B ARR with the majority from team/enterprise plans, but the motion was: free individual → team adoption → enterprise licensing. The *users* were often the same humans who started on free — they grew into enterprise buyers. It's both expansion AND genuine enterprise acquisition simultaneously.

#### Estimated Breakdown (Inferred, Not Directly Measured)

Based on the convergence of ARR mix data, NRR mechanics, and PLG pricing patterns:

| Strategy | Estimated % of PLG "Upmarket" Revenue |
|---|---|
| **Price increases / tier gating on existing SMB/mid-market users** | ~50–60% |
| **Seat/usage expansion within existing accounts (organic growth)** | ~20–25% |
| **Genuinely new enterprise logos acquired through enterprise GTM** | ~15–25% |

The first two categories — totaling **~75–80%** — involve extracting more revenue from the existing base, not acquiring genuinely new enterprise customers. Only **~20–25%** of what gets called "moving upmarket" represents true new-logo enterprise acquisition with dedicated sales motions.

#### Why This Matters

This has direct implications for M3 (sorting/information markets): the "moving upmarket" narrative is partly an **information asymmetry play** toward investors. Companies report ARPU increases and NRR improvements as evidence of enterprise traction, but the underlying mechanism is often price optimization on a captive base. Investors who can't decompose expansion-from-existing vs. new-logo-enterprise are systematically mispricing these companies.

It also connects to M1 (outcomes gap): if you're selling outcomes measurement to school districts, the "move upmarket" from individual teachers to district-wide contracts faces the same ambiguity. Are you genuinely winning new districts, or are you re-pricing existing teacher-level users into district SKUs?
