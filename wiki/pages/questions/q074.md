### The Question

Q072 found Denver's proficiency recovery rate was indistinguishable from (or slightly worse than) non-rationalizing peer districts. Two possible explanations:
1. **Mechanism wrong:** Rationalization genuinely doesn't affect outcomes — tool removal is offset by disruption costs
2. **Measurement wrong:** Annual proficiency snapshots are too coarse to detect the mechanism

Tonight I'm investigating both. The answer turns out to be **"measurement, but deeper than expected — proficiency is the WRONG METRIC entirely."**

---

### Part 1: The Growth Data Q072 Didn't Examine

Q072 used proficiency rates (% meeting/exceeding expectations) — a LEVEL metric. Colorado's CDE also publishes Median Growth Percentiles (MGP) — a TRAJECTORY metric that controls for starting point by comparing each student's progress to peers who started at the same achievement level.

#### Why This Distinction Is Critical

| Dimension | Proficiency (Level) | Growth (MGP) |
|-----------|-------------------|--------------|
| What it measures | Where students ARE | How much students MOVED |
| Controls for demographics | No — strongly correlated with SES, ELL status | Yes — compares to academic peers at same starting level |
| Sensitivity to composition changes | High — new ELL students directly lower % | Low — new students measured against similar peers |
| Detects school contribution | Poorly — conflates school effect with population | Better — isolates year-over-year progress |
| Time resolution | Annual snapshot | Annual, but measures within-year growth |

**Denver's demographic profile explains most of the proficiency gap:**
- Denver: ~36% ELL, ~60% FRL (free/reduced lunch)
- Douglas County: ~5% ELL, ~15% FRL
- Jeffco: ~8% ELL, ~30% FRL

A district with 7x the ELL proportion will ALWAYS show lower proficiency regardless of instructional quality. Growth controls for this by comparing each student to peers at the same starting level.

### Part 2: What the Growth Data Reveals

#### Finding 1: Denver's students are growing FASTER than peers — despite lower proficiency

Q072 showed Denver's proficiency lagging behind Jeffco and Douglas County. But the growth data tells the OPPOSITE story:

- Denver's ELA MGP (53 in 2025) MATCHES Jeffco (53) and Douglas County (53) at elementary level
- Denver's district-wide ELA MGP (55) EXCEEDS the state median (50) by 5 points
- Denver's district-wide Math MGP (53) exceeds state median by 3 points

**This means Denver's students are learning as much or MORE per year as suburban peers — they're just starting from a lower base because of demographic composition.**

The proficiency gap isn't about instructional quality — it's about where students enter. The growth data shows the district IS effectively teaching.

#### Finding 2: Possible "Disruption Dip" in 2024 — exactly during active rationalization

Denver's elementary ELA MGP trajectory: 52 → 52 → **51** → 53

The 2024 dip (51, down from 52 in 2023) coincides EXACTLY with the active rationalization year (2023-24 school year = peak tool cutting). The 2025 recovery to 53 (highest since 2018) coincides with post-rationalization stabilization.

**Caution:** A 1-point MGP change is within noise range. I CANNOT claim this is a real disruption dip. But the temporal alignment is suggestive and consistent with the disruption cost theory:

**Disruption Cost Mechanism (theoretical, UNCONFIRMED by this data):**
1. Teacher retraining — losing familiar interfaces, rebuilding lesson plans
2. Student re-adaptation — navigating new platforms, losing saved progress/content
3. Content loss — teacher-created materials on discontinued platforms
4. Routine disruption — breaking established workflows mid-transition
5. Recovery — new coherent ecosystem stabilizes, remaining tools get higher-fidelity usage

Expected signature in growth data: small MGP dip during transition year → recovery to equal or higher MGP afterward.

Observed: 1-point dip in 2024 → 2-point recovery in 2025. **Weakly consistent but not probative.**

#### Finding 3: 2025 Convergence — All Three Districts Hit MGP 53

In 2025, Denver, Jeffco, and Douglas County ALL converge to elementary ELA MGP of 53. This is likely regression toward the mean / measurement noise rather than meaningful. But it eliminates the prior narrative that suburban districts were "outperforming" Denver — that narrative was based on proficiency (wrong metric for this comparison).

#### Finding 4: DPS Subgroup Growth — Equity Dimension

From DPS 2024-25 annual report:
- Black students, Latine students, students with disabilities, and FRL students showed year-over-year MGP growth that OUTPACED White students and non-FRL students
- English Learners saw a 6-point year-over-year MGP increase in both Reading & Writing and Math

**This is notable post-rationalization.** If tool cutting disproportionately harmed disadvantaged students (who might rely more on supplemental digital tools), we'd expect DECLINING subgroup MGP. Instead, subgroup MGP INCREASED. This doesn't prove rationalization helped (many concurrent initiatives), but it rules out the worst-case scenario.

### Part 3: The Real Answer — Both Mechanism AND Measurement

#### Is the mechanism wrong?

**Partially.** Rationalization probably IS neutral on aggregate proficiency — the claim "cutting bad tools improves outcomes" is too simplistic. The actual mechanism is more nuanced:

- Cutting unused tools → zero outcome effect (nobody was using them anyway)
- Cutting used-but-ineffective tools → small positive effect (teachers redirect time to better instruction)
- Cutting used-and-helpful tools → negative effect (disruption costs exceed savings)
- Net effect → approximately zero, with high variance depending on what was cut

**The mechanism isn't wrong — it's undifferentiated.** A naive "cut everything" approach produces a null aggregate result because the effects cancel out. A TARGETED approach that cuts only unused/ineffective tools while preserving effective ones would produce a positive result — but that requires exactly the outcomes monitoring infrastructure that doesn't exist (the product).

#### Is the measurement wrong?

**Yes, fundamentally.** Annual proficiency snapshots are the WRONG metric for detecting rationalization effects because:

1. **Proficiency conflates demographics with instruction.** Denver's 36% ELL population structurally depresses proficiency. Any rationalization signal is buried under demographic noise.

2. **Even growth (MGP) is too coarse.** District-level MGP aggregates across all students regardless of which tools they used. The rationalization signal exists (if it exists at all) only for students who were USING the removed tools. District-wide MGP dilutes the signal with the ~80% of students whose tool ecosystem didn't change.

3. **The RIGHT metric doesn't exist yet.** What you need:
   - Which specific tools were removed
   - Which students were using those tools (usage data)
   - Those students' individual growth percentiles before/after removal
   - Compared to matched students whose tools were NOT changed
   - Controlling for tool quality (ESSA evidence tier) and dosage (hours of use)

**This is exactly what NWEA MAP + LearnPlatform usage data together would provide.** And it's exactly what nobody has built.

### Part 4: Product Insight — Predictive Disruption Cost Modeling

The research reveals a feature no existing product offers: **pre-cut disruption cost prediction.**

If a rationalization tool had granular usage data, it could model BEFORE cutting:
- "Cutting Tool X will affect 47 teachers who have 312 hours of content invested and 1,200 students mid-sequence"
- "Cutting Tool Y will affect 3 teachers with 0 hours of content — it's effectively unused"
- "Optimal cut schedule: remove Y immediately, phase out X at end of semester to minimize disruption"

**This transforms rationalization from "cut and pray" to "precision surgery."** The key inputs:
1. Usage depth (hours, frequency, content creation activity)
2. Student engagement patterns (are students mid-sequence or between units?)
3. Teacher workflow integration (is the tool embedded in daily instruction or used occasionally?)
4. Replacement availability (is there an equivalent approved tool, or does cutting leave a gap?)

**Nobody can build this without per-student, per-tool usage data linked to growth data — the exact data infrastructure the product would create.**

### Part 5: Revised Understanding of the Product Thesis

#### Before This Research (Q072 interpretation):
"Retrospective analysis from public data produces inconclusive results → prospective monitoring has a moat because the study is hard."

#### After This Research (Q074 revision):
"The study isn't just hard — it's asking the WRONG QUESTION with the WRONG METRIC. Proficiency is structurally incapable of detecting rationalization effects in demographically diverse districts. Growth is better but too coarse at the aggregate level. The product opportunity isn't just 'monitoring outcomes' — it's building the analytical infrastructure that makes INDIVIDUAL-LEVEL growth-conditional-on-tool-change analysis possible for the first time."

#### Three-Layer Product Architecture Emerging:
1. **Usage Layer** — which students use which tools, how much, how deeply (LearnPlatform does this)
2. **Growth Layer** — individual student growth trajectories (NWEA MAP does this)
3. **Decision Layer** — connect 1 + 2 to predict disruption costs, identify ineffective tools, optimize cut schedules (NOBODY does this)

Layer 3 is the product. Layers 1 and 2 are inputs from existing infrastructure.

### Methodology Notes

**Data sources (all public):**
- CDE SchoolView Growth Dashboard (schoolview.cde.state.co.us) — MGP by district, year, subject, student group

**Limitations:**
- Elementary-only CDE data may not reflect middle school patterns (DPS reports higher district-wide MGP)
- 1-point MGP changes are within measurement noise — the "disruption dip" observation is suggestive, not conclusive
- Cannot distinguish rationalization effects from concurrent DPS initiatives (HQIM adoption, Instructional Framework, tutoring programs)
- Denver Math MGP 2024-2025 elementary data incomplete (CDE page truncated)
- No Jeffco or Douglas County Math MGP comparison compiled due to time constraints

**Confidence levels:**
- "Proficiency is the wrong metric for rationalization evaluation" — HIGH confidence (structural argument + data demonstration)
- "Growth is a better metric" — HIGH confidence (established psychometric literature)
- "Disruption dip exists" — LOW confidence (1 data point, within noise)
- "Individual SGP conditional on tool change is the right metric" — HIGH confidence (theoretical) / ZERO evidence (nobody's done it)
- "The product should include predictive disruption cost modeling" — MEDIUM-HIGH confidence (novel feature derived from analysis)

### Summary

**The answer is BOTH mechanism and measurement, but measurement is the bigger problem.**

The mechanism isn't wrong — it's undifferentiated. Naive rationalization produces null aggregate results because positive and negative effects cancel out. Targeted rationalization (informed by usage + outcomes data) should produce positive results but has never been tested because the data infrastructure doesn't exist.

The measurement is fundamentally flawed. Proficiency is structurally incapable of detecting rationalization effects in demographically diverse districts. Growth (MGP) is better but too coarse at the aggregate level. The right metric (individual SGP conditional on tool change) doesn't exist because nobody has connected per-student usage data to per-student growth data across a rationalization event.

**The product thesis evolves:** Not just "outcomes monitoring" but "the analytical infrastructure that makes it possible to evaluate whether cutting a specific tool helped or hurt specific students." That's Layer 3 — the decision layer — and it's entirely greenfield.
