### Findings

### Short answer

**Yes for interview scorecards:** at least one accessible source explicitly says modern interview scoring sheets should include **“evidence notes”** and use the same **criteria, scale, and evidence notes** across candidates. That directly answers the “evidence / evaluator comments” part.

**Not proven for demo or bid-evaluation sheets from the provided sources:** the Freelancer pages and PDF excerpts were not text-readable enough to verify whether their scoring sheets mention validation, references, efficacy, outcomes, or implementation risk.

### Main finding

The strongest supported pattern is that scoring sheets are not just numeric rubrics; they are becoming **structured evidence-capture tools**. Kira’s guide says a good scoring sheet should help compare candidates using the same “criteria, scale, and evidence notes,” and should be specific enough to prevent “vague feedback” from driving decisions. This implies the evaluator comment field is meant to preserve the factual basis for a score, not merely record impressions.

### What is cited vs inferred vs unknown

- **Cited:** Interview scoring sheets can include criteria, scoring scales, and **evidence notes**.
- **Cited:** The stated purpose is to reduce vague feedback and support more consistent comparison.
- **Inferred:** This structure is adjacent to validation/outcomes thinking because it asks evaluators to anchor judgments in observable job-related evidence.
- **Unknown from supplied sources:** Whether demo scoring sheets explicitly include subcriteria for customer references, validation studies, efficacy, measured outcomes, implementation risk, or post-sale adoption risk.

### Implication for M1

If procurement/demo rubrics mirror interview scorecards, the product opportunity is not merely “better scoring.” It is **turning subjective evaluator impressions into auditable evidence packets**: claim → observed evidence → confidence → risk → outcome relevance. That is where evidence, validation, implementation risk, and publication/study design could become differentiating infrastructure rather than after-the-fact marketing.
