### Core Question

If different ESA member districts rationalize their EdTech portfolios at different times, does the staggered timing create a natural experiment suitable for causal inference? Specifically: can staggered difference-in-differences (Callaway & Sant'Anna 2021) or stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial (SW-CRT) designs be applied at the ESA level to produce ESSA Tier I or Tier II evidence for the Portfolio Growth Attribution methodology identified in Q076?

### Answer Summary

**Yes, staggered ESA rationalization is a viable natural experiment design — and the ESA structure is uniquely suited to it.** But the design choice determines the evidence tier, and the path to Tier I requires a feature no one has proposed: the product itself must be designed to GENERATE the research as a byproduct of its normal operation.

Two distinct design paths emerge:

1. **Observational staggered DiD** (retrospective, using existing data) → ESSA Tier II at best (quasi-experimental, WWC "meets standards with reservations"). Achievable with 20+ districts. Callaway & Sant'Anna estimators handle treatment heterogeneity.

2. **Stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial** (prospective, randomizing rollout order) → ESSA Tier I achievable (experimental, WWC "meets standards without reservations"). Requires 6+ districts with randomized rollout timing. The ESA structure makes this practical in ways that direct district recruitment doesn't.

**The key insight: the product's deployment model (phased rollout across ESA member districts) IS the research design.** The product doesn't just measure rationalization outcomes — its deployment schedule generates the variation needed for causal inference. This is a deep structural advantage: no competitor can produce this evidence without also having the ESA distribution channel (Q065). The product and the evidence strategy are the same thing.

### Detailed Analysis

#### 1. Why ESA Structure Maps to Research Design

A typical ESA (e.g., PSESD in Washington, Capital Region BOCES in New York, Region 4 ESC in Texas) serves 20-50+ member districts that share:
- Same state assessment system (common outcome measure)
- Similar policy environment (same state regulations, funding streams)
- Geographic proximity (reduces unobserved heterogeneity)
- **Different rationalization timelines** (each district has its own tech director, budget cycle, and political dynamics)

This creates the exact conditions Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021) require:
- **Multiple groups** (districts) adopting treatment (rationalization using the product) at **different times**
- **Never-treated** and **not-yet-treated** comparison groups available within the same ESA
- **Common outcome measures** (state assessments, NWEA MAP if available)
- **Pre-treatment parallel trends** testable from historical assessment data

No other organizational structure in K-12 education provides this combination. Individual districts are N=1. State-level analysis is too coarse. ESAs are the Goldilocks unit. [CITE: Q065 research — 553 ESAs across 39+ states serving ~95% of districts]

#### 2. Two Design Paths — Observational vs. Prospective

##### Path A: Observational Staggered DiD (Immediate, Lower Evidence Tier)

**What it is:** Use naturally occurring rationalization timing differences across ESA districts to construct a staggered DiD study. Districts that rationalized early = treated; districts that haven't yet = control.

**Methodology:**
- Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021) group-time ATT(g,t) estimators
- Goodman-Bacon (2021) decomposition to diagnose TWFE bias
- "Not-yet-treated" districts as control group (cleaner than "never-treated" because all ESA districts are likely to rationalize eventually)

**Requirements:**
- 20+ districts within the ESA to achieve adequate power [CITE: power analysis literature — 250+ units ideal for staggered DiD, but ESA districts have thousands of students per district, so cluster-level N=20 may suffice with large within-cluster samples]
- At least 3 pre-treatment time periods for parallel trends testing
- At least 2 post-treatment periods per cohort for dynamic effects
- Outcome data: state assessment scores (public) or NWEA MAP Growth (requires data sharing)

**ESSA Classification:** Quasi-experimental → ESSA Tier II (Moderate Evidence) if it meets WWC standards with reservations. Requires multi-site sample ≥350 students across ≥2 sites. A single ESA with 20 districts easily exceeds this.

**Strengths:**
- Can begin immediately with existing public data
- Doesn't require researcher control over timing
- Callaway & Sant'Anna handles heterogeneous treatment effects properly (unlike TWFE)
- Lower coordination cost — just needs data access

**Weaknesses:**
- Endogeneity of timing: districts that rationalize first may be systematically different (more organized, better leadership, different fiscal pressure). This threatens the parallel trends assumption.
- Cannot achieve Tier I without randomization
- Absorbing treatment assumption is imperfect (see Section 3)

##### Path B: Stepped-Wedge Cluster Randomized Trial (Prospective, Tier I Achievable)

**What it is:** When the product (rationalization platform) is deployed across an ESA's member districts, randomize the ORDER of deployment. All districts eventually get the product, but the rollout sequence is randomly determined. This creates a stepped-wedge CRT within the natural ESA structure.

**Methodology:**
- SW-CRT with districts as clusters, students as units of observation
- Randomize rollout in 3-5 "steps" (e.g., 4-6 districts per step per semester)
- All districts serve as controls before they receive the product, and as treated after
- Analyze using mixed-effects models accounting for clustering and secular time trends

**Requirements:**
- Minimum 6 districts per ESA (research suggests ≥2 districts per step, ≥3 steps) [CITE: BMJ guidance on SW-CRT design, Hemming et al. 2015]
- 45% of cohort SW-CRTs in published literature have <10 clusters — so 6-12 districts is feasible [CITE: Grayling et al. 2017 review]
- Pre-registration and IRB approval (or equivalent district-level data sharing agreement)
- 18-36 month study duration (2-3 academic years)

**ESSA Classification:** Randomized controlled trial → ESSA Tier I (Strong Evidence) if it meets WWC standards without reservations. Requires ≥350 students across ≥2 sites. A 6-district ESA with 3,000+ students per district (18,000+ total) far exceeds this threshold.

**Strengths:**
- **Randomization of rollout order eliminates selection bias** — the key threat to Path A
- **All districts eventually receive the product** — ethically sound (no indefinite control group), and commercially sound (ESA contract covers all districts)
- **Each district serves as its own control** (pre vs. post), AND as a between-group control for later-treated districts
- **The product deployment IS the research design** — no separate research infrastructure needed
- **Can produce ESSA Tier I evidence** — the gold standard that only 20 studies in the entire K-12 EdTech literature have achieved [CITE: Stanford SCALE 2026]

**Weaknesses:**
- Requires ESA cooperation on randomizing deployment order (may face resistance from eager districts or those with urgent needs)
- Longer timeline to full results (18-36 months)
- Statistical analysis is complex (need to account for clustering, secular trends, potential carry-over effects)
- Need to ensure fidelity of implementation is comparable across steps

#### 3. The Absorbing Treatment Problem — And Why EdTech Rationalization Breaks It

Standard staggered DiD assumes "absorbing treatment" — once treated, always treated. EdTech rationalization violates this in two ways:

**Violation 1: Tool removal is the treatment, not tool adoption.**
Most DiD studies in education examine the ADOPTION of a new program. Rationalization is the REMOVAL of tools. The "treatment" is subtraction, not addition. This is unusual in the literature but not methodologically problematic — the treatment variable is "underwent rationalization using the platform" (binary), and its effect on student growth is the estimand.

**Violation 2: Districts may re-adopt tools or experience treatment reversal.**
After rationalizing, a district might bring back previously cut tools due to teacher pushback, new funding, or administrative turnover. This breaks absorbing treatment.

**Solutions:**
- **de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfoeuille (2020)** developed DiD estimators specifically for non-absorbing treatments and treatment switching. Their `did_multiplegt` Stata/R package handles this directly. [CITE: de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfoeuille, "Difference-in-Differences Estimators of Intertemporal Treatment Effects," arXiv:2007.04267]
- **Intent-to-treat (ITT) analysis:** Define "treated" as "the platform was deployed in the district during this period" regardless of whether individual tools were actually cut. This preserves absorbing treatment (you can't un-deploy the platform) while measuring the practical effect of having the rationalization infrastructure available.
- **Treatment intensity models:** Rather than binary treated/untreated, use the NUMBER of tools rationalized (continuous treatment). de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfoeuille handle non-binary treatments explicitly.

The ITT approach is the cleanest: the product deployment is absorbing (districts don't un-subscribe from their ESA's shared platform), even if individual rationalization decisions are reversible. This maps exactly to what the product-as-research-engine requires.

#### 4. Power Analysis: What Sample Sizes Are Needed?

**For Staggered DiD (Path A):**
- Callaway & Sant'Anna `did` R package warns if any treatment group has <5 observations
- Literature suggests 250+ units for 80% power with 10-15% effect sizes
- But: district-level clusters contain thousands of students. A 20-district ESA with 3,000 students/district = 60,000 student-level observations. Within-cluster variation is enormous.
- **Realistic requirement:** 15-25 districts within an ESA, 3+ pre-treatment periods, 2+ post-treatment periods. The average ESA serves 20-50 districts. [SUFFICIENT]

**For Stepped-Wedge CRT (Path B):**
- Hemming et al. (2015, BMJ): Minimum ≥2 clusters per step, ≥3 steps → minimum 6 clusters
- 45% of published SW-CRTs have <10 clusters [CITE: review literature]
- Power depends heavily on ICC (intraclass correlation). For education outcomes, ICC typically 0.05-0.25 at district level. Lower ICC = more power per cluster.
- SW-CRT can be MORE powerful than parallel CRT when ICC is low and cluster sizes are large — exactly the ESA-EdTech scenario
- **Realistic requirement:** 8-15 districts, 3-4 steps over 2 academic years. [ACHIEVABLE within a single mid-size ESA]

**Critical finding:** The ESA structure provides adequate statistical power for BOTH designs. The bottleneck is not sample size — it's data access and cooperation.

**The evidence ladder is also the product roadmap:**
1. Launch with public data analysis (Tier III, proves concept) — Q072 already did this
2. Deploy to first ESA, collect observational data (Tier II, 12-18 months)

#### 6. The Product-as-Research-Engine Insight

**This is the deepest finding of Q078.** The standard model in EdTech evidence is:

1. Build product
2. Sell product
3. Commission external research firm (RAND, AIR, Mathematica) to study it
4. Hope for positive results
5. Use results in marketing

This model is expensive ($200K-$2M per study), slow (2-4 years), and the product has no structural advantage — any competitor can hire RAND too.

**The ESA-deployed rationalization platform reverses this:**

1. Build product with research infrastructure baked in
2. Sell to ESA (all member districts get access)
3. The deployment schedule IS the research design (randomized or naturally staggered)
4. The platform automatically collects the data needed for the study (usage → growth linkage)
5. Evidence is a BYPRODUCT of normal operation, not a separate cost center
6. Each new ESA deployment generates another independent replication

**Structural advantages of this model:**
- **Zero marginal cost of evidence generation** — the data collection happens as districts use the product
- **Replication is built into the business model** — each ESA is an independent study site
- **Competitors cannot replicate** without the same ESA distribution channel AND the same data infrastructure AND the same methodological framework
- **Evidence accumulates with each sale** — the 10th ESA deployment provides 10 independent study sites, approaching meta-analytic strength

This is what Q065's "ESA-as-customer" insight looks like when combined with Q076's "methodology-as-IP" insight. The distribution strategy and the evidence strategy are the same strategy.

#### 7. Who Has Done Something Like This? (Analogies)

**No one has done this for EdTech rationalization.** But analogous product-as-research designs exist:

1. **NWEA MAP Growth itself:** NWEA's product (adaptive assessments) generates the normative data that makes the product valuable. Each school that uses MAP Growth contributes to the norming sample. The product IS the research infrastructure. [CITE: NWEA normative data methodology]

2. **Khan Academy + College Board (SAT Prep):** Khan Academy's free SAT prep generates data on which practice methods improve scores, which feeds back into product improvement. The product produces research that validates the product. [CITE: College Board-Khan Academy partnership research]

3. **Outcomes-Based Contracting (OBC):** OBC contracts embed outcome measurement into the payment structure. The contract IS the evaluation mechanism. Q078's design extends this from single tools to portfolios. [CITE: Digital Promise OBC, Q066]

4. **Clinical trial networks (medicine):** Research networks like PCORI or NCI cooperative groups embed research into routine clinical practice. Patients receive treatment; treatment generates evidence; evidence improves treatment. The ESA rationalization platform would be the education equivalent of a learning health system. [ANALOGY — not identical]

**None of these analogies have been applied to EdTech rationalization.** The synthesis is novel.

#### 8. Methodological Challenges and Mitigations

| Challenge | Severity | Mitigation |
|-----------|----------|------------|
| Non-absorbing treatment (tools re-adopted) | Medium | ITT analysis (product deployment is absorbing); de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfoeuille estimators for non-binary treatment |
| Spillover between districts within ESA | Medium | ESA districts share ideas but don't share students. Cross-district contamination unlikely for student outcomes. More concerning for adoption decisions — but this is a BEHAVIORAL spillover, not an outcome spillover. |
| Parallel trends assumption | High | Testable with 3+ pre-treatment periods of assessment data. Within-ESA districts are more similar than cross-state comparisons (Q072 Denver vs. Jeffco). |
| Treatment effect heterogeneity across districts | High | Callaway & Sant'Anna ATT(g,t) explicitly handles this; group-time disaggregation reveals which district types benefit most |
| Secular time trends (state policy changes) | Medium | Within-ESA design controls for state-level changes (all districts face same state policy). SW-CRT explicitly models calendar time effects. |
| IRB/ethics of randomized rollout | Low | SW-CRT is ethically preferred — all districts receive the product, only the ORDER is randomized. No district is permanently denied access. Standard in public health rollouts. |
| Political resistance to randomized order | Medium | Frame as "phased implementation" rather than "randomized experiment." ESAs already phase rollouts for logistical reasons. Adding randomization to the phase order is a small change. |
| Sample size for subgroup analysis | Medium-High | Individual district may be too small for subgroup SGP analysis. ESA-level pooling across 20+ districts provides adequate power for ELL, FRL, SWD subgroups. |

#### 9. Connection to Q076 Methodology

Q076 identified the novel methodology: individual SGP conditional on tool portfolio change. Q078 identifies the DESIGN that makes this methodology executable:

```
Q076 (WHAT to measure) + Q078 (HOW to generate variation) = complete research design
```

Within each ESA district, Q076's methodology operates at the student level:
- Each student has a tool portfolio (set of EdTech tools they actively use)
- Rationalization changes the portfolio (tools removed, usage patterns shift)
- SGP measures each student's growth trajectory
- The model conditions SGP on portfolio change, controlling for demographics and prior achievement

Across ESA districts, Q078's staggered design provides the causal identification:
- Different districts rationalize at different times (natural variation) or in randomized order (experimental variation)
- The between-district variation identifies the CAUSAL effect of rationalization-with-platform vs. status quo
- The within-student variation (Q076) identifies WHICH aspects of portfolio change drive growth effects

**Together, they solve the multi-treatment attribution problem identified in Q076:** the staggered design provides exogenous variation in TIMING (why we believe the effect is causal), while the SGP-conditional model provides variation in CONTENT (which tools mattered for which students).
