### 1. The Procurement Pipeline — How Money Actually Moves

#### Procurement Tiers (typical, varies by state/district)

| Tier | Amount | Process Required | Approver | Timeline |
|------|--------|-----------------|----------|----------|
| Micro | $0 – $1,000 | PCard/petty cash | Principal or dept head | Days |
| Small | $1,000 – $5,000 | Quotes encouraged, not required | Site admin | 1-4 weeks |
| Medium | $5,000 – $25,000 | 2-3 written quotes | Deputy superintendent | 2-8 weeks |
| Large | $25,000 – $50,000 | Formal quotes or mini-RFP | Superintendent + board notification | 2-4 months |
| Major | $50,000+ | Full RFP/competitive bid | Board vote required | 4-12 months |

**CITED:** Ranges synthesized from multiple district purchasing manuals (Oregon SD, Pearland ISD, Washington County USD). California raised its formal bid threshold to $114,500 in January 2024 (ACSA). Federal micro-purchase threshold is $10,000. **INFERRED:** Most EdTech SaaS deals for small-to-mid districts fall in the $5K-$25K range per year.

#### The Critical Insight: Where the Sweet Spot Lives

For a new EdTech startup, the procurement sweet spot is **$5,000-$25,000/year per district**. Here's why:

- **Below $5K:** Easy to approve (principal/admin discretion), but too small to build a business. At $3K/district, you need 333 districts for $1M ARR. The 2-6 month sales cycle makes this math punishing.
- **$5K-$25K:** Often falls under admin discretion or needs only 2-3 quotes (no full RFP). A superintendent or deputy superintendent can approve without board vote in many districts. This is the "can sign without a meeting" zone.
- **Above $25K:** Triggers formal procurement (RFP, board vote). Sales cycle stretches to 6-12+ months. Requires being in the budget cycle (planned a year in advance). This is where enterprise EdTech companies (PowerSchool, Canvas) operate — and they have massive sales teams.

**INFERRED:** The ideal initial product should price at **$5-$15 per student per year**, which for a mid-size district (2,000-10,000 students) generates $10K-$150K per contract. A 5,000-student district at $10/student = $50K, which pushes into RFP territory. A 3,000-student district at $5/student = $15K, which lives in the sweet spot.

#### The Seasonal Gauntlet

EdTech purchasing follows a rigid annual cycle:

| Period | Activity | Implication for Vendor |
|--------|----------|----------------------|
| Aug-Oct | Teachers discover tools organically | Free/freemium gets in the door |
| Oct-Dec | IT/curriculum review, pilots requested | Must be ready for structured evaluation |
| Jan-Mar | RFPs issued, vendor presentations | Formal sales motion required |
| Apr-Jun | Budget finalization, purchase decisions | The "buy or wait another year" moment |
| Jul-Aug | Implementation and onboarding | Must have deployment support ready |

**CITED:** Multiple sources (K12Prospects, EdTech purchasing guides, Digital Promise). **Critical constraint:** If you miss the Jan-Mar window, you wait a full year. This means the effective "selling season" is about 6 months, and the decision window is narrower still.

#### Post-ESSER Fiscal Cliff (March 2026 Context)

**CITED:** ESSER III obligation deadline was September 30, 2024. Liquidation deadline was January 28, 2025, with some states extended to March 30, 2026 (approved extensions). Federal K-12 funding projected to decrease ~22% between 2024-25 and 2025-26, representing roughly $24B loss.

**INFERRED:** This is both headwind and tailwind:
- **Headwind:** Districts are cutting, not buying. New EdTech purchases face intense scrutiny. "Why do we need this?" is the default response.
- **Tailwind:** Districts that bought EdTech with ESSER money now face renewal decisions. Products that SAVE money or CONSOLIDATE existing tools have a stronger pitch than ever. The winning frame is not "buy something new" but "replace three things you're paying for with one thing that costs less."

### 2. The Conflict of Interest Constraint — This Is Not Optional

#### Washington State Law (RCW 42.23)

**CITED:** Washington's Code of Ethics for Municipal Officers (Chapter 42.23 RCW):
- School board members are classified as "municipal officers"
- General prohibition: no beneficial interest, direct or indirect, in any contract made by/through/under their supervision
- "Contract" is defined broadly: includes any sale, lease, purchase, or employment agreement

**Exception that DOESN'T apply to Ric:** RCW 42.23.030 permits contracts under $3,000/month — BUT this exception **explicitly does not apply to first-class school districts** (≥2,000 students).

**CITED:** First-class school district = 2,000+ enrolled students (RCW 28A.300.065). **UNKNOWN:** What is Ric's district's enrollment? If it's ≥2,000 (which most suburban WA districts are), the $3K/month exception is off the table entirely.

#### What This Means Practically

| Action | Legal Status | Risk |
|--------|-------------|------|
| Sell software to own district | **PROHIBITED** | Contract void, $500 fine, potential forfeiture of office |
| Influence others to buy your product for your district | **PROHIBITED** | Same penalties |
| Recuse yourself from the vote | **INSUFFICIENT** — prohibition is on having the interest, not on voting | Still violating RCW 42.23 |
| Sell to OTHER districts | **PERMITTED** | No conflict — you have no supervisory role |
| Use knowledge gained from board service to design better products | **GRAY AREA** | Likely fine (knowledge ≠ contract), but needs to feel clean |
| Resign from board, then sell to former district | **PERMITTED** after a reasonable cooling period | Should verify WA cooling-off provisions |

#### The Strategic Implication

**Ric cannot sell to his own district while serving on the board.** Period. Even if the product is genuinely the best option. Even if he recuses from the vote. The law prohibits the *interest*, not the *participation*.

This doesn't kill the EdTech strategy — it reshapes it:

1. **Ric's district is a design partner, not a customer.** He can observe problems, understand workflows, identify pain points — all as part of his board duties. He cannot monetize that relationship directly.
2. **First 10 customers must come from OTHER districts.** The board position provides credibility ("I serve on a school board, I built this because I saw the problem firsthand") without the legal liability.
3. **The network is the asset, not the contract.** Board members know other board members. WSSDA (Washington State School Directors' Association) conferences, regional meetings, inter-district committees — these are the distribution channels.
4. **Timeline decision:** If the EdTech product reaches significant scale, Ric may need to choose between board service and the company. This is a bridge to cross later, but it's worth knowing it's on the road.

### 3. The Awkward Economics of K-12 EdTech

#### The Varlamov Thesis (Source: Sasha Varlamov, former CEO of Coding Rooms)

**CITED** from detailed analysis:

- K-12 software TAM: ~$17B (vs. $1.3T enterprise IT spend, or $40B CRM alone)
- That $17B covers EVERYTHING: LMS, SIS, ERP, curriculum, assessments, cybersecurity, networking
- Per subsegment, you struggle to find $1B+ addressable markets (the VC sweet spot)
- K-12 sales = enterprise motion at SMB prices. The worst combination.
- Only two proven unicorn archetypes: (1) broad-use instructional platforms (Newsela, Kahoot, Nearpod) or (2) enterprise infrastructure (PowerSchool ~$5B, Canvas/Instructure ~$4B)
- Most niche EdTech is "a one-way road to zombie land (aka lifestyle business)"
- Monthly subscriptions are "simply not a thing" — schools can't handle them
- 43% of software license investments are wasted (unused/redundant tools)

#### Reality Check for Q039's EdTech Path

Q039 projected getting to $500K-$1M ARR and exiting at 12-25x AI-premium multiples. Let's stress-test:

| Metric | Optimistic | Realistic | Pessimistic |
|--------|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| ACV per district | $25K | $10K | $5K |
| Districts needed for $500K ARR | 20 | 50 | 100 |
| Sales cycle per district | 3 months | 6 months | 12 months |
| Time to 50 districts (solo) | 2 years | 3.5 years | Never |
| AI-premium exit multiple | 25x | 15x | 8x |
| Exit value at $500K ARR | $12.5M | $7.5M | $4M |

**INFERRED:** Getting to $500K ARR in K-12 EdTech as a side project is a 3-5 year grind, not a 12-18 month sprint. The sales cycle is the binding constraint, not the product development. And this assumes a product that genuinely solves a priority problem.

### 4. The Two Viable K-12 Product Strategies

#### Strategy A: Bottom-Up Freemium → District Upsell (Kahoot/ClassDojo Model)

**How it works:**
1. Free tool for individual teachers (zero procurement friction)
2. Teachers adopt organically, become internal advocates
3. Premium features require district-level license ($5-15/student)
4. Internal champion sells to admin for you

**Advantages:**
- No sales team needed initially
- Product-market fit validated by usage data before you sell anything
- Bypasses the procurement gauntlet entirely at first
- Board knowledge helps design something teachers actually want

**Disadvantages:**
- Free → paid conversion is hard (~2-5% in most freemium models)
- You need massive free adoption (10,000+ teachers) to generate meaningful paid conversion
- Takes 2-3 years to reach critical mass
- Teachers are the hardest demographic to monetize — they're used to everything being free

**Fit with Ric's constraints:** MODERATE. Tommy can build the product, but marketing and community-building take sustained effort. Ric's limited time is less of a constraint here because the product does its own selling — IF it's good enough.

#### Strategy B: Enterprise Sales to District Admins (Focused Niche)

**How it works:**
1. Identify a specific, acute pain point (e.g., school board agenda analytics, compliance tracking, parent communication)
2. Build a focused tool that replaces a manual process
3. Sell directly to superintendents/district administrators
4. Use board network for warm introductions to other districts

**Advantages:**
- Higher ACV ($10K-$50K per district)
- Fewer customers needed
- Ric's credibility as a board member is directly relevant
- Solves a problem Ric personally understands

**Disadvantages:**
- Enterprise sales motion with SMB budgets (Varlamov's trap)
- Ric must be the salesperson (relationships are the moat) — Tommy can't do this
- Each sale requires meetings, demos, pilots, procurement cycles
- Limited by Ric's available hours

**Fit with Ric's constraints:** HIGH strategic fit, LOW bandwidth fit. This is the right product type but needs Ric's time — the scarcest resource.

#### The Hybrid: What Actually Makes Sense

**INFERRED (synthesis):**

The optimal path combines both:
1. **Tommy builds a tool** that solves a real admin/board problem (identified through Ric's insider knowledge)
2. **Free version** for individual board members or small districts (bottom-up adoption)
3. **Paid district license** with additional features (analytics, multi-user, integrations)
4. **Ric makes 5-10 warm introductions per quarter** to superintendent contacts from WSSDA network
5. **AI does the heavy lifting:** automated onboarding, self-serve demos, data migration tools
6. **Ric never sells to his own district** — full stop

The product needs to be in a category where:
- The pain is acute enough that admins seek solutions proactively
- AI provides genuine differentiation (not just "EdTech with AI marketing")
- The post-ESSER consolidation wave creates demand ("replace 3 tools with 1")
- Price fits the $5K-$25K admin-discretion sweet spot

### 5. The Timeline Reality

#### Best Case (Moderate Scenario from Q039)

| Month | Milestone | Revenue |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| 0-3 | Problem identified, MVP built (Tommy-heavy) | $0 |
| 3-6 | 5 free pilots with districts (Ric intros) | $0 |
| 6-12 | 3-5 paid districts from pilot conversions | $15K-$75K ARR |
| 12-18 | Referral network producing warm leads | $50K-$150K ARR |
| 18-24 | 15-20 districts, product-market fit confirmed | $150K-$300K ARR |
| 24-36 | 30-50 districts, consideration for hire #1 or agency | $300K-$500K ARR |
| 36-48 | Potential acquisition interest or Series A path | $500K-$1M ARR |

**Key dependency:** This assumes Ric can dedicate 5-10 hours/week to sales and relationship-building. If actual availability is 2-3 hours/week, stretch everything by 50-100%.

#### The 2026-2027 Calendar Constraint

If we start product development now (March 2026), the earliest we can realistically enter the selling season is **January 2027** (for school year 2027-28 purchases). That means:
- March-December 2026: Build + 2-3 informal pilots (free)
- January-March 2027: First formal sales pitches
- April-June 2027: Budget decisions for 2027-28
- July-August 2027: First paid implementations
- **First revenue: ~Q3 2027 (16-18 months from now)**

This isn't pessimistic — it's the structural reality of the seasonal procurement cycle. Any plan that shows K-12 revenue in less than 12 months is lying.

### Summary

**What I learned:**
1. K-12 procurement has a narrow $5K-$25K "admin discretion" sweet spot that avoids formal RFPs
2. Washington state law explicitly prohibits Ric from selling to his own district — no exceptions likely apply to a first-class district
3. Post-ESSER, districts are cutting, which creates a "consolidation" opening but also makes new purchases harder
4. The seasonal calendar means first revenue is 16-18 months away at minimum
5. The Varlamov thesis is sobering: K-12 EdTech is "enterprise sales without enterprise budgets"
6. The viable path is hybrid: free tool for bottom-up adoption + paid district licenses via warm network

**What surprised me:**
- The conflict of interest constraint is more absolute than expected. Recusal doesn't fix it. The prohibition is on *having the interest*, not on participating in the decision. This fundamentally changes how the "unfair advantage" works — it's knowledge and network, not direct sales access.
- The 43% wasted software licenses statistic. The post-ESSER consolidation pitch ("we'll save you money by replacing redundant tools") might be more powerful than a net-new product pitch.

**What this changes:**
- Q039's "12-25x AI-premium exit" needs to be discounted for K-12 realities. EdTech acquisitions rarely exceed $5B (PowerSchool is the ceiling). A more realistic exit for a $500K-$1M ARR K-12 SaaS is 8-15x, putting the range at $4M-$15M — which is still transformative but more honest.
- The "Ric sells to his district" mental model is legally impossible. The strategy must be "Ric designs for his district's problems, sells to everyone else."
