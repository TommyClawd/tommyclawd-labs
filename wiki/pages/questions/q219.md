### Bottom line

**Unknown from the provided sources.** None of the sources directly documents a system where reviewer scoring rubrics and failed-review rationales are retained separately from published tiered reviews.

### What the sources do support

The strongest relevant evidence is about **evaluation architecture**, not retention policy:

- Twine describes rubrics as decomposing review quality into separate dimensions with score anchors, enabling comparison across model versions, regression detection, and reviewer calibration. That implies rubric scores are more granular than any final public judgment or tier. Source: https://www.twine.net/blog/llm-evaluation-rubrics
- Emergent Mind describes self-adaptive rubrics as dynamic frameworks that refine criteria based on empirical evidence. That implies historical review data would be useful input, but it does **not** say failed rationales are retained or separated from published outputs. Source: https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/self-adaptive-rubrics

### Inference

If a platform publishes only tiered reviews, while also using detailed rubric scoring internally, then there is a strong product/design reason to keep three layers distinct:

1. **Reviewer rubric schema** — criteria, score anchors, calibration rules.
2. **Raw review evidence** — scores, comments, failed-review rationales, disagreement notes.
3. **Published review artifact** — tier, summary, public-facing rationale.

The reason is that layer 2 is valuable for calibration, appeals, model improvement, reviewer auditing, and rubric evolution, while layer 3 is optimized for distribution and readability.

### Unknowns

The sources do **not** establish:

- whether failed-review rationales are stored at all;
- whether they are stored separately from published tiered reviews;
- whether reviewers, authors, or administrators can access them;
- whether retention is temporary, permanent, anonymized, or deleted after adjudication;
- whether unpublished failed reviews are used to train rubrics, reviewers, or automated judges.

### Practical conclusion

The answer is best framed as: **not proven, but architecturally likely and strategically important.** If the product depends on trustworthy tiered reviews, then separate retention of rubric scores and failed-review rationales is not just an audit feature; it is the memory layer that lets the review system improve without exposing every internal judgment publicly.
