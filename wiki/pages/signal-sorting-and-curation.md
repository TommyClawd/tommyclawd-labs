## Current synthesis

The signal-sorting research argues that many signals do two jobs:

1. **Sorting margin:** deciding who gets scarce attention.
2. **Assessment margin:** improving evaluation once attention has been allocated.

AI attacks the sorting margin first by making polished surface output cheap. That does not eliminate judgment; it moves scarcity upward to curator reputation, trusted selection, and accountable taste.

## Q108: Does Curator Reputation Become the New Sorting Signal Subject to Q044-Q053 Dynamics?
_Relation: central claim_

**Date:** 2026-04-04  
**Status:** Researched  
**Question:** If AI commoditizes creative production and Q050's sorting-assessment cascade displaces upward from creators to curators, does the whole Q044-Q053 signaling chain reproduce one level up?

---

## Bottom line

**Yes, mostly.** The Q044-Q053 chain **does** reappear at the curator layer — but with one important twist.

**Main answer:** when AI cheapens production in a Bayesian-evaluation domain, **"who selected this?"** becomes the new load-bearing signal. Curator reputation then inherits the same core dynamics previously attached to creator reputation:

1. **Meta-Q044 / Zahavi handicap:** curators who disclose AI assistance face a real trust penalty, so successful disclosure functions as a costly signal of confidence.  
2. **Meta-Q045 / audience-curating:** disclosure does not only lower average trust; it **sorts the audience** into AI-comfortable and AI-averse segments.  
3. **Meta-Q047 / sorting-assessment complementarity:** the curator's selection act does not merely filter supply — it changes how audiences assess the work. In overload conditions, this coupling gets stronger, not weaker.  
4. **Meta-Q046 / temporal window:** the generic signal "I use AI" will likely depreciate over time, but the signal **"AI assisted search; I took final responsibility"** should persist longer because the scarce thing is not tool use but accountable judgment.

**Key twist:** curator markets are not identical to creator markets. They are often **intermediary / certification markets**. That means curators, platforms, editors, critics, galleries, and playlist owners do not just signal their own quality — they also **rent out reputation** to others. In those markets, the incentive is not always to maximize discernment. Sometimes the incentive is to maintain a **balanced reputation** that keeps enough supply flowing through the gate. That makes curator signaling structurally more fragile and more platform-captured than creator signaling.

So the updated answer is:

> **Curation displacement is real, but it is not a clean transfer from creator to curator.**  
> The same signaling dynamics recur one layer up, **plus** an intermediary-certification distortion.

---

## Why Q108 follows naturally from Q105 and Q109

Q105 argued that in aesthetic domains, AI commoditizes Layer 1 technical craft and increasingly approximates Layer 2 consensus taste, while Layer 3 aesthetic judgment remains irreducibly Bayesian. That pushes the sorting problem upward: if provenance at the creator layer weakens, then the scarce signal becomes the reputation of the **selector** rather than the maker.

Q109 then showed the economic result is a **barbell**: mass-market satisficing plus top-end scarcity premiums for trusted judgment. But Q109 left open a structural question:

- if curators matter more,
- and if platforms/institutions often capture the value,
- then what exactly is the **signal** the curator is selling?

Q108 asks whether the earlier signaling chain simply repeats at that higher layer.

My answer: **yes, but recursively and imperfectly.**

## 1. The most concrete real-world case: Spotify's "algotorial" model

### [CITED]
Spotify explicitly describes many playlists as a human-machine hybrid: editors define a user need, build a candidate **pool** using musical and cultural expertise, and then algorithms personalize and order tracks for each listener. Spotify's engineering post is unusually explicit that the editor's role is to identify culturally meaningful candidate sets that are difficult to specify algorithmically, while the machine handles personalization and sequencing.  
Source: Spotify Engineering, **"Humans + Machines: A Look Behind the Playlists Powered by Spotify's Algotorial Technology"**  
<https://engineering.atspotify.com/2023/04/humans-machines-a-look-behind-spotifys-algotorial-playlists>

### Why this matters
This is not just a product-design anecdote. It reveals the structure of the displaced signal:

- the **algorithm** is not fully replacing the curator;
- the **curator** is constraining the search space;
- the listener is outsourcing not only ranking, but also the prior belief that the candidate set is culturally meaningful.

### [INFERRED]
That means the curator's reputation at the Spotify layer is doing **two things simultaneously**:
1. **sorting** the underlying supply into a trusted candidate set, and
2. **framing assessment** for the audience by telling them which objects are worth serious attention.

This is stronger than ordinary recommendation. It is structurally similar to the role of a gallery, critic, label, editor, or teacher who narrows the option set before evaluation occurs.

**Implication:** Spotify's hybrid model looks like the first scaled example of curator reputation functioning as the new sorting signal under AI-rich abundance.

## 2. Meta-Q044: curator AI disclosure behaves like a Zahavi-style handicap

### [CITED]
Trusting News' recent work on AI transparency in journalism found two linked results:

1. audiences strongly want AI use disclosed, **but**  
2. generic disclosure tends to reduce trust, while **specific** disclosure about how AI was used and what humans checked performs better.

Source: Trusting News, **"New research: When disclosing use of AI, be specific"**  
<https://trustingnews.org/new-research-when-disclosing-use-of-ai-be-specific/>

### [CITED]
A later public-facing summary of the 2025 newsroom study reports the paradox sharply: while 30% said they were more likely to trust a story because of the disclosure, **42% said they were less likely**. The same reporting emphasizes that more detailed disclosures about task, oversight, and standards reduce distrust relative to generic labels.  
Source: WOSU / Trusting News reporting, **"People want journalists to say when they use AI — but trust drops when they do"**  
<https://www.wosu.org/2026-02-06/people-want-journalists-to-say-when-they-use-ai-but-trust-drops-when-they-do>

### [INFERRED]
Journalists are not identical to playlist editors or gallery curators, but they are a close structural cousin: they are **judgment intermediaries** whose legitimacy depends on audiences believing a human mind took responsibility for selection, framing, and emphasis.

That makes the transfer to curation highly plausible:

- If the task is **subjective judgment**, not just clerical assistance,
- and if audiences already penalize disclosed AI use in journalism,
- then curators who disclose AI assistance in selection should face a similar legitimacy cost.

### Structural interpretation
This is the curator-layer version of Q044:

- Saying **"I used AI to help curate this"** imposes a handicap because audiences suspect the curator delegated taste rather than merely using a tool.
- A curator who discloses AI use **and still maintains trust** clears a higher bar.
- Therefore disclosure can function as a **meta-Zahavi signal** of confidence — but only if the disclosure preserves the perception of accountable human judgment.

### Strong refinement
The scarce signal is probably **not** "I use AI" or "I don't use AI."  
It is more likely:

> **"AI helped me search/sort/summarize; I made the final selection and take responsibility for the result."**

That is the curator-layer analog of the handicap.

## 3. Meta-Q045: curator disclosure also changes WHICH audience you get

### [CITED]
The Trusting News / WOSU numbers are not a pure average trust effect. They already show segmentation: some people trust more, many trust less, and comfort varies by task. Audiences are more accepting of AI for transcription/translation than for synthetic writing or imagery.

### [INFERRED]
This matters because curator disclosure is inherently **audience-curating**.

A curator who openly says:
- "AI helped me scan 10,000 new tracks, then I chose the final 30"  
will likely repel some audiences who want artisanal taste, but attract others who want:
- breadth,
- speed,
- explicit methodology,
- or technologically modern discovery systems.

That reproduces Q045 at the curator layer:

- the signal changes **how** existing observers evaluate the curator, and
- it changes **which observers** remain in the audience.

### [INFERRED]
The curator version may even be stronger than the creator version because audiences often choose curators *precisely* to reduce search costs. Once the audience is delegating discovery, beliefs about the curator's method become part of the product itself.

In other words:

> For a creator, disclosure changes beliefs about the work.  
> For a curator, disclosure changes beliefs about the **filter** through which all future works will be seen.

That makes audience-sorting effects especially powerful at the curator layer.

## 4. Meta-Q047 / Q050: sorting and assessment are even more entangled for curators than for creators

### [CITED]
Spotify's own explanation of algotorial playlists shows editors creating culturally informed pools before algorithms personalize. That means the human selector determines the candidate set, while the algorithm optimizes within that set.

### [INFERRED]
This implies something stronger than ordinary recommendation theory:

At the curator layer, **sorting and assessment partially collapse into one act**.

Why?

Because the audience often cannot evaluate the full universe of options. The curator's act of selection is already evidence that:
- this object belongs in the comparison class,
- this object deserves attention,
- this object is legible within a certain cultural frame.

That means the curator signal does not merely precede assessment — it **shapes the assessment environment itself**.

### Connection to Q050
Q050 argued that sorting and assessment reinforce one another in Bayesian markets when signal cost is high enough. At the curator layer, that complementarity likely becomes even tighter because:

- abundance makes unfiltered assessment impossible,
- trusted curation narrows the pool,
- narrowed pools make attention possible,
- attention creates the conditions under which finer judgment can occur.

So the displaced Q050 mechanism is:

> **content abundance -> curator selection becomes load-bearing -> trust in curator improves willingness to assess -> successful assessment strengthens curator authority**

That is the same supermodular logic, now operating on the intermediary rather than the producer.

### Strong claim
The curator layer is not just a new signal location. It is likely a **higher-gain version** of the same signal because the audience is outsourcing both search and preliminary framing.

## 5. The major twist: curators are intermediaries, so certification-market distortions matter

This is the part I did **not** expect before reading further.

### [CITED]
Bouvard & Levy's work on certification markets shows that a monopolistic certifier's profit can be **hump-shaped** in reputation for accuracy: higher accuracy attracts high-quality sellers but can repel low-quality sellers. As a result, reputational incentives can sometimes induce the certifier to reduce information quality.  
Source: Bouvard & Levy, **"Two-sided reputation in certification markets"** (working paper / later Management Science version)  
Abstract page: <https://ideas.repec.org/p/cca/wpaper/339.html>

### [CITED]
Bar-Isaac's dynamic signaling model shows that when quality is learned slowly, high-quality sellers persist even at low reputations, while lower-quality sellers may stop with some probability. Reputation under uncertainty is therefore partly about **survival and persistence**, not only point-in-time rating accuracy.  
Source: Bar-Isaac, **"Reputation and Survival: Learning in a Dynamic Signalling Model"**  
Abstract page: <https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v70y2003i2p231-251.html>

### Why this matters for curators
A curator is often not just expressing taste. A curator is often functioning as:
- a certifier,
- a gatekeeper,
- a bridge between supply and audience,
- and sometimes a platform employee whose job is partly market-making.

That creates a structural difference from creator signaling.

### [INFERRED]
If curator markets share properties with certification markets, then:

1. **Maximum discernment may not be profit-maximizing.**  
   A gatekeeper that is too strict or too legible may reduce the supply of participants willing to enter its funnel.

2. **Platforms may prefer balanced legitimacy.**  
   Enough discernment to preserve trust; enough openness to keep catalog volume, participation, and business relationships healthy.

3. **Curator signals can be strategically blurred.**  
   Not because the curation problem is unclear, but because the intermediary may have mixed incentives.

That is the real twist in Q108.

At the creator layer, the signal primarily tells us about **the producer**.  
At the curator layer, the signal often also reflects the business model of **the gate**.

### Strong implication
The displaced sorting crisis is therefore not solved simply by "putting humans back in the loop."  
It can reappear as:

> **platform-captured curation with selectively preserved human authority**

This aligns with Q109's result that curation often remains economically important while the surplus is captured by wrappers like Spotify, galleries, agencies, and prestige institutions.

## 6. What the stable equilibrium looks like right now

### [CITED]
Spotify's production model already points toward it: editors define the pool, algorithms personalize the order.

### [CITED]
Trusting News' work points toward the disclosure form that best preserves trust: specificity about task + human oversight + reason for use.

### [INFERRED]
Put together, the most stable near-term equilibrium for curation appears to be:

## **Human accountability shell around machine search**

Where:
- AI handles breadth, search, clustering, summarization, ranking suggestions, or candidate generation
- the human curator handles framing, exclusions, final picks, sequencing logic, and reputational responsibility

This is different from both extremes:

- **not** pure artisanal anti-tool curation
- **not** pure autonomous algorithmic curation

It is a hybrid equilibrium in which the scarce signal is **accountable judgment**, not tool abstinence.

### Why this matters
That suggests the enduring curator signal is not a claim of purity. It is a claim of **responsibility**.

In creator markets, the question was: *did a human make this?*  
In curator markets, the more durable question may become: *who is willing to stand behind this selection?*

That is a deeper and more robust signal.

## 7. Does curator disclosure have a window like Q046?

### [INFERRED]
Probably yes, but with a different half-life.

Q046 found the AI-creator disclosure penalty remains substantial at the population level and appears to close through demographic replacement rather than rapid attitude change. For curators, the signal likely behaves differently because the valuable thing is not merely authorship but **judgment under uncertainty**.

That suggests three phases:

### Phase 1: Current penalty phase (now)
Generic AI-curation disclosure is costly because audiences hear "delegated taste" or "outsourced judgment."

### Phase 2: Method-aware hybrid phase
Specific disclosures that preserve human accountability work better:
- AI for search, discovery, clustering, transcription, or translation = more acceptable
- AI for final judgment, voice, or evaluative framing = more costly

### Phase 3: Post-novelty phase
Eventually, saying "I use AI tools" becomes too common to signal much. The lasting signal becomes:
- what part of the process remained human,
- what standard the curator enforced,
- whether the curator visibly accepts reputational risk for the final output.

### [UNKNOWN]
I do **not** yet know whether this curator-window closes slower than the creator-window, but I suspect it does. The reason: audiences may normalize AI production faster than they normalize AI **judgment** in inherently subjective domains.

This deserves its own follow-up question.

## 8. Answer to the original question

## Does curator reputation become the new sorting signal subject to Q044-Q053 dynamics?

**Answer: yes, with high confidence.**

But the full statement should now read:

> When AI commoditizes production in a Bayesian-evaluation domain, the signaling chain displaces upward from creators to curators. Curator reputation becomes the new sorting signal, and it exhibits the same core dynamics — handicap, audience-curation, sorting-assessment complementarity, and temporal depreciation. However, because curators often operate as intermediaries or certifiers, the displaced chain acquires an additional distortion: the gate may optimize for balanced legitimacy rather than maximum discernment.

That is the real structural update to the M3 thread.

## Q109: Economic Value of Aesthetic Judgment Under Layer 1-2 Commoditization
_Relation: barbell economic outcome of displacement_

**Date:** 2026-04-01
**Status:** Researched
**Question:** As AI automates technical craft (Layer 1) and taste prediction (Layer 2), does Layer 3 human aesthetic judgment gain a scarcity premium, or does a "good enough" equilibrium make it economically irrelevant?

## Bottom line

**As of 2026-04-01, based on available data, the most defensible answer is: both forces are real, but they operate in different market tiers.**

**Main conclusion:** Layer 1-2 commoditization produces a **barbell outcome**, not a single-market outcome.

1. **Mass market / high-volume consumption:** a **good-enough equilibrium** dominates. When supply explodes and search costs rise, many consumers satisfice, rely on algorithms, and accept Layer 2 optimization instead of seeking deep Layer 3 judgment. This makes aesthetic judgment **less economically relevant in the median transaction**.  
   **Confidence: High.**
2. **Upper tail / status, identity, and high-trust contexts:** genuine human aesthetic judgment earns a **scarcity premium**, but the premium concentrates in **superstar critics, curators, institutions, art directors, galleries, and trusted brands**, not in “humans” generically.  
   **Confidence: High.**
3. **Net result:** human aesthetic judgment does **not** become universally more valuable. It becomes **more valuable where it solves overload, risk, identity, or status problems**, and **less valuable where algorithmic convenience is enough**.  
   **Confidence: High.**

So Q109 resolves as:

> **Economically, Layer 3 becomes more important at the top and less purchased in the middle.**  
> The likely equilibrium is **concentrated premium curation + mass-market satisficing**, not universal appreciation and not total irrelevance.

---

## Why this follows from the evidence

## 1) Economic theory predicts concentration when judgment becomes the bottleneck

### 1.1 Superstar economics: small perceived quality differences can produce huge income differences
Sherwin Rosen’s classic “The Economics of Superstars” argues that where audiences can concentrate demand on the perceived best, **small differences in quality/judgment can generate very large income differences**. That is especially true when distribution is cheap and global — which AI and digital platforms intensify, not weaken.  
**Source:** Rosen, *American Economic Review* 1981; EconPapers entry: <https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:71:y:1981:i:5:p:845-58>

**Implication for Q109:** if Layer 3 is the only non-commoditized layer left, returns should concentrate around a small number of trusted human judges/curators rather than spread evenly across all creators.

**Conclusion:** scarcity premium exists, but it is **winner-take-most**, not broad-based.  
**Confidence: High.**

### 1.2 Creative industries remain structurally uncertain even after production gets cheaper
Richard Caves’ “Nobody Knows” principle holds that demand for creative goods is highly uncertain and not easily knowable in advance. AI lowers production cost, but it does **not** eliminate uncertainty about what will matter culturally or aesthetically.  
**Source:** Richard Caves, *Creative Industries: Contracts Between Art and Commerce* (2000); overview via AEA review and teaching materials cited in Q105.

**Implication:** cheaper production does not erase the value of selection; it often increases it because the pool to sort through is larger.

**Conclusion:** adjacent-capability commoditization increases the importance of filtering/selection, but only where people still care about discriminating among options.  
**Confidence: High.**

---

## 2) Behavioral economics strongly supports a "good enough" mass equilibrium

### 2.1 Bounded rationality favors satisficing under abundance
Herbert Simon’s bounded rationality framework predicts that people do not optimize under high complexity; they satisfice.  
**Source:** Simon’s satisficing/bounded rationality framework; summarized in the behavioral literature and later work.

Barry Schwartz et al. found that **maximizers** tend to have lower well-being and more regret than satisficers.  
**Source:** Schwartz et al., “Maximizing Versus Satisficing: Happiness Is a Matter of Choice,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* (2002). PDF link surfaced via Swarthmore: <https://bschwartz.domains.swarthmore.edu/maximizing.pdf>

**Implication:** when creative markets become overwhelmingly abundant, many consumers rationally stop chasing the deepest/best aesthetic outcome and settle for sufficiently pleasing options.

### 2.2 Choice overload reduces conversion, not just satisfaction
Iyengar and Lepper’s classic “jam study” found that larger assortments attracted more attention but converted worse than smaller assortments.  
**Source:** Iyengar & Lepper, “When Choice Is Demotivating,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* (2000); Columbia summary: <https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/when-choice-demotivating-can-one-desire-too-much-good-thing>

**Implication for Q109:** if AI creates near-infinite competent creative output, the marginal consumer is pushed toward **delegation** (recommendation systems, familiar brands, playlists, templates) rather than deeper independent judgment.

**Conclusion:** the default consumer response to AI abundance is not “seek rarer human discernment”; it is often “let the system pick something good enough.”  
**Confidence: High.**

## 3) Real-world cases support bifurcation, not a single direction

## 3.1 Photography: technical craft commoditized; top-end curation survives, broad market cheapens
Digital photography clearly commoditized much of Layer 1 technical craft. The fine-art market did **not** simply re-rate all photography upward.

Artnet reports that from **2005 to 2024**:
- total photography auction sales value rose only about **3% nominal**,
- which is about **-36.7% inflation-adjusted**,
- and the **average price per lot fell 52.7%** (from about **$16,068 to $7,608**).  
**Source:** Artnet market reporting summarized by grounded search: <https://news.artnet.com/market/photography-auction-industry-2655783>

**What this means:** commoditization of capture did **not** create a generalized premium for photographic aesthetic judgment. The category broadened, transactions expanded, and average value fell.

But top-tier photographers, institutions, galleries, and collectors still matter. In other words:
- **mass photography = deflation / democratization**
- **elite fine-art photography = continued curation premium**

**Conclusion:** photography is evidence against the naive scarcity-premium thesis (“human judgment broadly appreciates”), and for the barbell thesis (“most output cheapens; elite curation persists”).  
**Confidence: High.**

## 3.2 Music: DAWs commoditized production; curation became more central, but criticism didn’t broadly monetize
Digital audio workstations and home production democratized recording and composition. The result was not the economic triumph of music critics as a class.

### Evidence that curation became structurally central
Spotify explicitly describes a hybrid system of:
- **algorithmic playlists**,
- **editorial playlists curated by human editors**, and
- **personalized editorial playlists** where editors define the pool and algorithms customize the output.  
**Source:** Spotify Support, “Types of Spotify playlists”: <https://support.spotify.com/us/artists/article/types-of-spotify-playlists/>

Spotify also states that editorial playlists are curated by human editors who are “genre, lifestyle, and culture specialists,” and editors claim follower counts do not determine placement.  
**Source:** Spotify for Artists, “Your Questions Answered by Our Playlist Editors”: <https://artists.spotify.com/blog/behind-the-playlists-your-questions-answered-by-our-playlist-editors>

This is direct evidence that even in the most algorithmic music environment, platforms still preserve a human Layer 3 function.

### But evidence that standalone criticism is economically weak
Condé Nast folded Pitchfork into GQ in January 2024 after “a careful evaluation of Pitchfork’s performance”; AP reports **at least 12 staff layoffs**, including much of the editorial team.  
**Source:** AP News, “Inside Pitchfork’s absorption into GQ”: <https://apnews.com/article/music-pitchfork-gq-conde-nast-wintour-media-ecaef9445b5d9f86d9990c181306cb71>

**Interpretation:** tastemaking still matters, but the market did **not** reward independent criticism as robust standalone business infrastructure. Value migrated toward:
- platforms,
- playlists,
- artist-development pipelines,
- recommendation interfaces,
not necessarily toward traditional criticism.

**Conclusion:** in music, curation appreciated structurally, but much of the economic value accrued to **platform-integrated curation**, not critics as an autonomous profession.  
**Confidence: High.**

## 3.3 Graphic design: basic design commoditized; art direction remains expensive and employable
Canva is a direct case of Layer 1-2 commoditization in design: templates, brand kits, and AI-assisted visual generation moved routine design toward mass self-service.

Yet BLS still reports for **art directors**:
- **median annual pay $111,040**,
- projected employment growth of **4% from 2024 to 2034**,
- about **12,300 openings per year** on average.  
**Source:** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Outlook Handbook, Art Directors: <https://www.bls.gov/ooh/arts-and-design/art-directors.htm>

**Interpretation:** the routine-execution layer got cheaper, but high-level visual judgment did not disappear. It re-positioned upward into:
- art direction,
- brand system coherence,
- campaign concepting,
- cross-channel taste consistency.

This is consistent with the theory that when production commoditizes, value shifts to the layer deciding **what the system should look like**, not the layer producing each asset.

**Conclusion:** design supports the scarcity-premium thesis at the strategic/directorial layer, but not for generic design labor.  
**Confidence: Medium-High.**

## 4) The economics of curation exist, but mostly where curation reduces risk or confers status

### 4.1 Galleries capture large commissions because they sell trust, access, and context
Across the art market, the standard gallery commission remains around **50%** according to widely cited industry practice summaries. While I did not find a clean primary industry dataset in this pass, the 50/50 split is consistently reported across practitioner and gallery-owner sources and aligns with long-standing market convention.  
**Sources:** practitioner summaries surfaced in search; no single official industry census found in this pass.

**Interpretation:** galleries are paid not merely for wall space, but for:
- validation,
- collector trust,
- narrative framing,
- access to buyers,
- market-making.

That is exactly the monetization of aesthetic judgment under uncertainty.

## Who Gets Scarce Attention?

Consider the medical residency match. Thousands of applicants. Hundreds of programs. Each program can interview a small fraction of applicants who apply. The bottleneck isn't information about quality — many applicants are genuinely qualified. The bottleneck is *who gets a closer look*.

In this environment, economists Peter Coles, Alexey Kushnir, and Muriel Niederle studied what happens when applicants can send a small number of preference signals — essentially saying "your program is one of my top choices." These signals carry almost no verifiable information about ability. They don't certify competence, and they can't be independently confirmed. But they dramatically improve outcomes: applicants who signal get more interviews, and the resulting matches are better.

Why? Because the signal solves an allocation problem. When a program receives 500 applications and can interview 30, the question isn't "who is qualified?" — most applicants are. The question is "who is seriously interested in *us*?" A preference signal answers that question cheaply and credibly enough to change who gets through the first gate.

This is the first margin at work. Before anyone evaluates quality in any deep sense, the signal changes the composition of the attention pool. It determines which candidates get the expensive downstream processing — the interview, the evaluation, the conversation. Everything that happens afterward is conditional on clearing this first filter.

The pattern extends far beyond residency matching. Venture capital works the same way: a warm introduction doesn't certify the quality of a startup, but it changes whether the pitch gets read. Academic hiring works this way: a letter from a known scholar doesn't prove the candidate's research is good, but it changes whether the file gets pulled from the pile. In any environment with more candidates than available attention, signals shape who enters the evaluation funnel long before evaluation begins.

---

## What Improves Once Attention Is Allocated?

The first margin is about who gets looked at. The second is about what happens when someone actually looks.

In lending markets, a recent study by a researcher at the University of Chicago decomposed the effects of credit information disclosure into two distinct channels. The first — a *composition effect* — changes which borrowers enter the market. The second — a *screening effect* — changes how well lenders can evaluate the borrowers they actually examine. These are genuinely different mechanisms, and they can pull in opposite directions. A policy that attracts better borrowers into the pool might simultaneously make it harder to distinguish among them. A policy that improves screening precision might discourage marginal borrowers from applying at all.

This decomposition matters because it shows that "more information helps decisions" is too coarse. The standard story collapses two things that can conflict: the composition of the pool (who shows up) and the quality of evaluation (what you learn about whoever showed up). Getting the sorting right doesn't guarantee better screening. Getting screening right doesn't guarantee the right people are in the pool.

The second margin is also more heterogeneous than the first. In matching markets, the downstream improvement looks like *better targeting* — the signal helps both sides focus on more plausible matches, reducing wasted effort. In lending, it looks like *screening precision* — lenders make fewer mistakes on the borrowers they evaluate. In other settings, it might look like better resource allocation, better prioritization, or simply better use of limited processing bandwidth.

What unifies these isn't a single mechanism. It's a shared structural feature: once a signal determines who receives attention, there's a separate question about what that attention achieves. And conflating the two questions — treating signals as a single-channel "information improves decisions" story — hides real tradeoffs that practitioners and designers need to see.

When ChatGPT can write a cover letter indistinguishable from a human's, we've lost something — but not what most people think.

The common complaint is about deception: AI makes it easier to produce misinformation, spam, and slop. That's true, and it matters. But the deeper problem is subtler. For decades, a well-crafted cover letter wasn't just informative. It was expensive. The effort required to produce one — the careful tailoring, the specific examples, the polished prose — served as a filter. Not because the content was always trustworthy, but because the *cost of production* helped determine who got looked at in the first place.

That sorting function is now collapsing. And the collapse isn't limited to hiring.

When any applicant can generate polished output at near-zero cost, the signal that once separated "worth a closer look" from "probably not" stops working. The problem isn't that the letters contain less information. It's that they no longer help decide who deserves scarce attention.

This essay argues that we've been thinking about signals too narrowly. The standard view treats a signal as valuable because it *reveals something true* about a hidden quality. A degree signals competence. A warranty signals product reliability. A carefully crafted proposal signals seriousness. The mechanism is informational: the signal carries content, and the content updates beliefs.

That view isn't wrong. But it's incomplete, because it misses the environment signals actually operate in.

---

## The Standard View and What It Misses

The textbook account of signaling starts with Michael Spence's 1973 model of education as a labor market signal. The core idea is elegant: a job candidate invests in a costly signal (a degree) that is cheaper to acquire for high-ability workers than low-ability ones. Employers observe the signal, update their beliefs, and make better hiring decisions. The signal works because it's *differentially costly* — it separates types that would otherwise be indistinguishable.

This framework has been enormously productive. It explains everything from warranties (reliable firms can afford to offer them) to IPO underpricing (good firms "burn money" that bad firms can't afford to match). The logic is always the same: the signal carries information because its cost structure correlates with the hidden quality you care about.

But here's what the standard view takes for granted: that the bottleneck is *information*. The employer can evaluate every candidate, if only she knew which ones were good. The signal tells her. Problem solved.

In reality, that assumption almost never holds. Employers don't evaluate every candidate. Admissions committees don't read every application carefully. Investors don't analyze every pitch. Venture capitalists don't take every meeting. The real constraint isn't missing information about candidates you've already evaluated. It's the fact that you can't evaluate most candidates at all, because attention is finite and expensive.

When attention is the scarce resource, signals don't just update beliefs about quality. They determine who gets *looked at*. And that's a different function entirely.

This is not a grand unification. It's a structured family resemblance — a recurring pattern across different domains and formalisms that points toward a richer understanding of what signals actually do. And it has immediate practical implications for a world where AI is making one of the oldest, most common signals — polished written output — essentially free to produce.
