Q047 companion explainer

Signals don’t just reveal information.
They allocate attention.

The common view of signaling says a signal matters because it tells you something true about hidden quality. The stronger view is that many signals do two jobs: they help decide who gets looked at, and then they improve what happens once attention is allocated. AI threatens the first function fastest by making polished output cheap.

Scarce attention, not just scarce information
Sorting margin → who gets looked at
Downstream margin → what improves after attention
Cheap polish breaks triage before it breaks truth
Question 1

Who receives scarce attention?

In crowded systems, the first problem is often not “who is best?” but “who gets a closer look at all?” Preference signals, warm introductions, polished proposals, and effort-heavy applications often function as triage devices before deep evaluation starts.

Question 2

What improves once attention lands?

After attention is allocated, different mechanisms improve different things: targeting, match quality, decision quality, or screening precision. This second margin is real, but more heterogeneous than the first.

The collapse mechanism

Cheap polish breaks the sorting layer first

If polished output used to be costly, it helped filter who deserved scarce attention. Once AI makes polished output cheap, the content may still carry information — but the triage function weakens or collapses.

01
Old equilibriumPolished writing is costly, so it helps sort serious candidates from low-effort ones.
02
Cost collapseAI makes tailored, polished output fast and cheap for almost everyone.
03
Triage failureEverything now clears the old “looks serious” bar, so attention allocation gets noisier.
04
Downstream degradationEven better screening tools struggle because the pool reaching evaluation changed.
Strong support

Matching markets

Coles / Kushnir / Niederle show that preference signals help determine who receives scarce attention and improve downstream targeting in crowded matching environments.

Strong support

Lending / screening

Kim separates composition and screening effects. That is the clearest evidence that pool composition and evaluation quality are distinct channels.

Partial support

Attention management

Lipnowski / Mathevet / Wei do not prove the same decomposition, but they strongly support the neighboring premise that attention is scarce and more information can crowd out better processing.

Design implication

Don’t ask only “does the signal contain information?”

  • Does it still sort who deserves scarce attention?
  • What remains costly or commitment-bearing?
  • What friction was doing useful triage work?
Warning

Better evaluation won’t fully save broken sorting

  • If the wrong pool reaches the expensive stage, assessment improvements arrive too late.
  • Systems often fail at triage before they visibly fail at truth.
  • The next durable signals will likely be rationed, costly, or harder to fake.
Plain-language takeaway
The problem with cheap signals isn’t only that they tell us less.

It’s that they make it harder to know what deserves attention in the first place.
